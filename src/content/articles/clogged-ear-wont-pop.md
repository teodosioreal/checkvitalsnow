The plane landed three hours ago. You've swallowed, yawned, chewed gum on the drive home, and pressed on your ear more times than you can count. Your own voice still sounds like it's coming from inside a fishbowl.

**A clogged ear that won't pop is most often caused by one of three things: earwax, a Eustachian tube that isn't opening properly after a cold, allergies, or a flight, or fluid behind the eardrum.** Most cases clear up on their own or with simple, gentle steps. A few need a doctor, and one rare cause, sudden hearing loss, needs one quickly.

It's an irritating feeling but a very common one. Below is a table to match your clues to the likely cause, what may help, the things that tend to make it worse, and the signs that mean it's time to get your ear looked at.

## What "popping" your ear actually does

Your middle ear sits behind the eardrum. It connects to the back of your nose and throat through a narrow channel called the Eustachian tube. Each time you swallow or yawn, that tube briefly opens to let air in or out, so the pressure on both sides of your eardrum stays even. That little click is the "pop."

When the tube is swollen from a cold or allergies, or can't keep up with fast pressure changes on a plane, the pressure stays uneven. The eardrum gets pulled or pushed, and you feel fullness, muffled hearing, and sometimes pain.

Earwax is a different story. It blocks the ear canal from the outside, so no amount of swallowing will move it. That's why figuring out the cause matters before you try anything.

## Clogged ear clues: match the cause to the next step

Look at what came before the blocked feeling and what comes with it.

| Possible cause | Clues that point to it | Reasonable next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Earwax buildup** | Came on gradually, or suddenly after a shower or swim; no cold; maybe itching; little or no pain | Softening drops if your eardrum is healthy; a doctor can remove stubborn wax |
| **Eustachian tube not opening** (after a cold, sinus infection, or allergies) | Stuffy nose; crackling or popping sounds when you swallow; fullness in one or both ears | Swallowing, yawning, treating the congestion; see a doctor if it lasts beyond a couple of weeks |
| **Airplane ear** (also after diving or mountain driving) | Started during descent or a fast change in altitude; pressure or pain | Swallow, yawn, chew gum; call a doctor if pain is severe or it lasts more than a few hours |
| **Fluid or middle ear infection** | Often follows a cold; ear pain, fever, or fluid draining; common in children | See a doctor |
| **Outer ear infection (swimmer's ear)** | Itching or pain that gets worse when you tug the earlobe; recent swimming | See a doctor; keep the ear dry |
| **Sudden hearing loss** | Hearing dropped over hours or a day in one ear, sometimes with a pop, ringing, or dizziness; no cold or flight to explain it | See a doctor right away, ideally the same day |

If several rows sound right, start with the gentlest steps and give yourself a short deadline. If it isn't better within a week or two, or if you're in the last row, get checked.

## Gentle things that may help a blocked ear

### For pressure from a cold, allergies, or a flight

These help the Eustachian tube open on its own:

- **Swallow often.** Sipping water or sucking on a hard candy makes you swallow more.
- **Yawn,** even a fake one. A wide yawn can open the tube.
- **Chew gum,** especially during a plane's descent.
- **Try a gentle blow.** Mayo Clinic describes pinching your nostrils, closing your mouth, and gently blowing as if blowing your nose, then swallowing. The key word is gentle. Forceful blowing can hurt the ear.
- **Treat the congestion.** Rest and fluids may make you feel more comfortable while a cold runs its course. Ask a pharmacist or doctor before using decongestant medicines, especially if you have high blood pressure or heart problems, or for a child.

### For earwax

MedlinePlus explains that earwax is normal and protective, and ears usually clean themselves. When wax does build up:

- **Softening drops** may help loosen it over several days. MedlinePlus mentions options such as mineral oil, baby oil, glycerin, or over-the-counter wax drops.
- **Don't use drops or water rinses** if you have, or might have, a hole in your eardrum, ear tubes, or past ear surgery, or if your ear hurts or is draining. Ask a doctor first.
- **A clinician can remove it** with tools, suction, or gentle rinsing if home steps don't help. This is quick and usually comfortable.

## What to stop doing to a clogged ear

A blocked ear is maddening, and the urge to dig is strong. These habits often make things worse:

- **Cotton swabs.** Mayo Clinic notes that swabs and similar objects can push wax deeper and pack it against the eardrum. Clean only the outer ear with a washcloth.
- **Hairpins, keys, or fingernails.** These can scratch the ear canal, which may lead to infection.
- **Ear candles.** Ear candling means placing a lit, hollow cone in the ear. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has warned against it, citing burns to the face and ear, ear canal blockage from candle wax, and punctured eardrums.
- **Hard nose blowing or forceful "popping."** Too much pressure can injure the eardrum.
- **Flying or diving with a bad cold,** when you can avoid it. Mayo Clinic notes that congestion raises the risk of airplane ear. If you must fly, use the swallowing and yawning tricks during descent.
- **Waiting weeks with muffled hearing in one ear and no clear reason.** That's the one situation where patience can cost you.

## Paths by situation

### If it started on or right after a flight

Airplane ear usually eases within hours as you keep swallowing and yawning. Mayo Clinic advises seeing a doctor if discomfort, fullness, or muffled hearing lasts more than a few hours, or if you have severe pain, fluid or bleeding from the ear, or hearing loss.

### If it started during or after a cold

The Eustachian tube can stay swollen for a while after the rest of the cold is gone. Fullness that slowly improves is typical. Fever, worsening pain, or drainage suggests an infection that a doctor should check. MedlinePlus notes that ear infections are common after colds, especially in children.

### If you swim or wear earbuds or hearing aids for hours

Water can make wax swell and suddenly block the canal, and devices in the ear can push wax inward. Swimmer's ear is also more likely after time in the water. If the ear hurts when you tug on it, see a doctor.

### If it's your child

Young children get ear infections more often than adults. Tugging at the ear, fussiness, trouble sleeping, or fever are reasons to call the pediatrician.

## Signs your blocked ear needs a doctor

Make an appointment if you have:

- A blocked feeling that **lasts more than a week or two** or keeps coming back
- **Ear pain** that is severe or getting worse
- **Fever,** or fluid, pus, or blood **draining** from the ear
- **Dizziness or spinning** along with the fullness
- New **ringing** in the ear that doesn't fade
- A history of eardrum problems, ear tubes, or ear surgery

**Get care right away** if hearing in one ear dropped suddenly, over a few hours or overnight. NIDCD notes that people sometimes mistake sudden hearing loss for a blockage from allergies, a sinus infection, or earwax, and that delaying treatment can make it less effective.

**Call 911** if a blocked ear or dizziness comes with a severe headache, face drooping, weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, or confusion.

## Tonight's plan for a stubborn clogged ear

Before bed, look back at the table and name your most likely cause. If it's pressure from a cold or flight, swallow, yawn, and try one gentle blow, then give it time to settle. If it's wax and your eardrum is healthy, start softening drops and put the cotton swabs away for good. Set yourself a deadline: if it isn't clearly better in a week, or if your hearing dropped suddenly, call your doctor.
