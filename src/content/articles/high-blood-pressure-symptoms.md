"I'd know if my blood pressure were high. I'd feel it." It's one of the most common beliefs about blood pressure, and it's the reason so many people skip checks for years.

**Most people with high blood pressure have no symptoms at all.** You can feel completely fine while the extra force strains your heart, blood vessels, kidneys, and brain. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) notes that high blood pressure usually has no warning signs, so the only way to know is to measure it.

That isn't a reason to worry. It's a reason to check. Below you'll find the myths that fool people, the rare signs that mean an emergency, a quick look at who is most at risk, and a home log you can start this week.

## Why high blood pressure symptoms rarely show up for years

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries. When that force stays high, your heart works harder and your arteries slowly take damage. Your body doesn't send a pain signal for this kind of strain, so nothing feels wrong.

Over time, MedlinePlus explains, uncontrolled high blood pressure can lead to heart attack, stroke, heart failure, kidney disease, and vision problems. These problems can be the first time some people learn their pressure was high. Catching it early, through a simple cuff reading, gives you the chance to act long before that.

## Blood pressure myths: headaches, red face, nosebleeds, and more

Plenty of people assume certain feelings are their blood pressure "talking." The American Heart Association (AHA) has looked at the most common ones:

| What people think is a sign | What the AHA says |
| --- | --- |
| Headaches | High blood pressure generally does not cause headaches, except during a hypertensive crisis |
| Nosebleeds | Not a reliable sign, except in a hypertensive crisis |
| Red or flushed face | Facial flushing can happen with heat, exercise, alcohol, or spicy food, but high blood pressure is not the cause |
| Dizziness | Not caused by high blood pressure itself, though it can be a side effect of some blood pressure medicines. Sudden dizziness with loss of balance needs urgent care |
| Feeling nervous or tense | Stress can raise blood pressure for a short time, but feeling calm doesn't mean your numbers are normal |
| Blood spots in the eyes | More common in people with high blood pressure or diabetes, but neither one causes them |

The takeaway: how you feel is not a blood pressure reading. Someone with a pounding headache may have normal numbers, and someone who feels great may be in Stage 2. Our [blood pressure chart](/heart-health/blood-pressure-readings-chart/) explains what each range means.

## Hypertensive crisis symptoms that need 911

There is one situation where high blood pressure does cause symptoms: a hypertensive crisis, when pressure rises very high, very fast. The AHA describes it as a reading higher than 180 for the top number and/or higher than 120 for the bottom number.

If you get a reading that high, wait a minute and measure again. Then:

**Call 911 right away if your reading is still above 180/120 and you have any of these:**

- Chest pain
- Shortness of breath
- Back pain
- Numbness or weakness
- Changes in vision
- Difficulty speaking

These can be signs of damage to organs happening right now. Don't wait to see if it settles, and don't drive yourself.

**If the reading is still above 180/120 but you have none of those symptoms**, contact your doctor right away. The AHA calls this a situation that needs prompt medical attention, even when you feel fine.

Some of these signs overlap with a heart attack and a stroke. It helps to know those well too: see our guides to [heart attack warning signs](/heart-health/heart-attack-warning-signs/) and [stroke signs](/heart-health/stroke-signs/).

## Who is most at risk for high blood pressure

High blood pressure is very common. The CDC estimates it affects nearly half of adults in the United States. Some people are more likely to develop it than others. The NHLBI lists these risk factors:

### Things you can't change

- **Age.** Blood pressure tends to rise as you get older, because blood vessels stiffen over time.
- **Family history.** High blood pressure often runs in families.
- **Race and ethnicity.** Black adults in the U.S. develop high blood pressure more often and often at a younger age.
- **Sex.** Men are more likely to develop it at younger ages, while women's risk rises later in life.

### Things you can work on

- A diet high in sodium and low in potassium-rich foods like fruits and vegetables
- Little physical activity
- Extra body weight
- Drinking too much alcohol
- Smoking
- Ongoing stress and short sleep

### Health conditions that raise risk

- **Diabetes** and **chronic kidney disease**
- **Sleep apnea**
- **Pregnancy.** Some people develop high blood pressure during pregnancy, including preeclampsia, which needs close follow-up.

If several of these apply to you, regular checks matter more. Our guide on [how to lower blood pressure](/heart-health/how-to-lower-blood-pressure/) covers the habits health agencies recommend.

## Paths by profile: how often to check your blood pressure

There is no single rule that fits everyone, so ask your doctor how often you should measure. As a starting point:

### If you've never had a high reading

Get your blood pressure checked at your regular checkups. Many pharmacies have a free machine, but use it as a quick screen, not a diagnosis.

### If you've had one high reading

One reading doesn't diagnose anything. Your doctor will usually want more readings, on different days, before deciding. A home log (below) is the most useful thing you can bring.

### If you're already taking blood pressure medicine

Home readings help your doctor see whether the dose is right. Never stop or change your medicine because a reading looks good. Talk to your doctor first.

### If you're pregnant or just had a baby

Tell your care team about any high reading, headache that won't go away, vision changes, or swelling. These need prompt attention during pregnancy and the weeks after birth.

## A home blood pressure log you can copy

A log turns scattered readings into a pattern your doctor can trust. The AHA recommends measuring at the same times each day, taking more than one reading a minute apart, and recording the results.

Copy this into a notebook or your phone's notes app:

| Date | Time | Reading 1 | Reading 2 | Arm used | Notes (meds taken? coffee? stress? symptoms?) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | 7:30 a.m. | 138/86 | 134/84 | Left | Before coffee, took meds after |
| | | | | | |

A few tips to keep your log useful:

- **Measure before coffee, exercise, or a cigarette**, and after resting for a few minutes with your back supported and feet flat.
- **Use the same arm** each time.
- **Write it down right away**, even if the number looks strange.
- **Bring the log, and your monitor, to your next visit**, so your doctor can check that the cuff fits and reads correctly.

The exact steps for an accurate reading are in our [blood pressure chart guide](/heart-health/blood-pressure-readings-chart/).

## Your blood pressure check this week

Pick one day this week to get a reading, whether at your doctor's office, a pharmacy, or with a home cuff. Write down the number, the time, and what you were doing beforehand. That single entry is the start of your log, and it's more than most people know about their own heart.
