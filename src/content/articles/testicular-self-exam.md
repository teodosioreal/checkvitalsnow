Myth: if something were wrong down there, it would hurt. In reality, the most common first sign of testicular cancer is a lump that doesn't hurt at all, which is one reason some men find it late.

**A testicular self-exam means gently rolling each testicle between your thumb and fingers, usually after a warm shower, to learn what's normal for you and notice any new lump, swelling, or change.** It takes a few minutes. Most lumps turn out to be something other than cancer, but a doctor should check any new one. Feeling awkward about this is normal. Doctors examine testicles every day, and to them it's a routine part of men's health.

## Testicular cancer: who it affects and why self-checks matter

Testicular cancer is not common overall, but it tends to strike younger men. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) notes it is the **most common cancer in men ages 15 to 35**. NCI also notes that it can usually be cured.

NCI lists these risk factors:

- A testicle that did not move down into the scrotum at birth (undescended testicle)
- Abnormal development of the testicles
- A personal history of testicular cancer
- A family history of testicular cancer, especially in a father or brother
- Being white

Having a risk factor doesn't mean you'll get it, and many men who do have none of these.

## What the USPSTF says about testicular self-exams

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) **recommends against routine screening** for testicular cancer in teens and adult men who have no symptoms. That includes exams by a doctor and self-exams. The task force found that screening is unlikely to add much benefit, partly because the cancer is uncommon and treatment works well even when the cancer is found because of symptoms.

So, why learn the exam at all? Because **knowing what's normal for your body** makes it easier to notice a real change. MedlinePlus describes the self-exam as a way to check for testicular cancer and explains how to do it. If you have a higher risk, such as a history of an undescended testicle, ask your doctor whether regular checks make sense for you.

## How to do a testicular self-exam in six steps

These steps follow the method described by MedlinePlus.

1. **Pick a warm moment.** During or right after a warm shower or bath, the scrotum relaxes and it's easier to feel what's inside.
2. **Stand up.** Many men find it easier to stand in front of a mirror. Look for any swelling or change in the skin of the scrotum.
3. **Check one testicle at a time.** Hold it gently with both hands, thumbs on top and index and middle fingers underneath.
4. **Roll it slowly** between your thumbs and fingers. Feel the whole surface. A normal testicle feels smooth, firm, and egg-shaped, without hard lumps.
5. **Find the epididymis.** This soft, tube-like structure sits at the top and back of each testicle. It's normal, and it's often mistaken for a lump. Knowing where it is helps you avoid a false alarm.
6. **Repeat on the other side** and compare. It's common for one testicle to be a little larger or hang lower than the other.

Use light pressure. The exam shouldn't hurt. If it does, that's worth mentioning to a doctor.

### Keep a simple self-check note

A short note on your phone helps you track what you find, especially if you notice something and want to recheck before an appointment.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

## Lump on a testicle: possible causes and next steps

Many conditions cause lumps or swelling in the scrotum, and most are not cancer. This table helps you understand the possibilities, not diagnose yourself. A doctor usually confirms the cause with an exam and an ultrasound.

| What you notice | Possible cause | What it usually is | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soft, lumpy swelling above the testicle, often on the left, like a bundle of cords; may feel heavier after standing | **Varicocele** | Enlarged veins in the scrotum | Regular doctor visit; mention any ache or fertility concerns |
| Smooth swelling around a testicle that makes the scrotum look bigger, usually painless | **Hydrocele** | A buildup of fluid around the testicle | Regular doctor visit to confirm |
| Pain, swelling, and warmth at the back of the testicle, sometimes with fever or burning when urinating | **Epididymitis** | Inflammation of the epididymis, often from an infection | See a doctor within a day or two; infections need treatment |
| Small, smooth, movable lump above or behind the testicle, separate from it | **Epididymal cyst (spermatocele)** | A fluid-filled sac, usually harmless | Regular doctor visit to confirm |
| Firm lump or hard area **on** the testicle, often painless; or a change in size or firmness | **Testicular tumor** | Could be cancer and needs testing | See a doctor promptly, within days to a couple of weeks |
| Bulge in the groin that may extend into the scrotum, bigger when you cough or strain | **Inguinal hernia** | Part of the intestine pushing through a weak spot | Doctor visit; emergency if the bulge becomes painful, hard, or can't be pushed back |
| Sudden, severe pain in one testicle, often with swelling, nausea, or vomiting | **Testicular torsion** | The testicle twists and cuts off its own blood supply | **Emergency: go to the ER or call 911 now** |

NCI lists a painless lump or swelling in either testicle, a change in how a testicle feels, a dull ache in the lower belly or groin, a sudden buildup of fluid in the scrotum, and pain or discomfort in a testicle or the scrotum as possible signs of testicular cancer. Other conditions cause these too, which is exactly why a doctor's exam matters.

## Self-exam tips by age and situation

### If you're a teen or in your 20s

This is the age group where testicular cancer is most common, but it's still rare. Learn your normal now. If you had an undescended testicle as a child, tell your doctor; it's a known risk factor.

### If you found something and you're worried

Don't keep checking it every hour. Write down what you felt and where, and call your doctor's office. Say "I found a new lump on my testicle." Offices treat that as a reason for a prompt visit. Most lumps turn out not to be cancer.

### If you're older

Testicular cancer is less common after middle age, but scrotal swellings like hydroceles and hernias become more common. New swelling at any age deserves a check. Your yearly visit is a good time to ask, along with the tests in our [men's health screenings by age](/mens-health/mens-health-screenings-by-age/) checklist.

## What happens at the doctor's visit for a testicular lump

Your doctor will ask about your symptoms and gently examine the scrotum, often while you stand and lie down. Common next steps:

- **Scrotal ultrasound.** A painless scan that shows whether a lump is solid or filled with fluid, and whether it's on or next to the testicle.
- **Blood tests.** NCI notes that certain tumor markers in the blood can help diagnose testicular cancer.
- **Urine tests** if an infection is likely.

If cancer is suspected, a urologist takes over care. If you have pain when urinating or pelvic pain rather than a lump, other conditions such as an infection of the prostate may be involved. Our guide to [prostatitis vs. an enlarged prostate](/mens-health/prostatitis-vs-enlarged-prostate/) explains the differences.

## Testicle pain and swelling that can't wait

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room right away** if you have:

- Sudden, severe pain in a testicle or the scrotum
- Pain with nausea, vomiting, or a testicle that sits higher than usual
- Severe pain or swelling after an injury
- A painful, hard bulge in the groin that won't go back in

MedlinePlus explains that testicular torsion is a surgical emergency. Fast treatment, within hours, gives the best chance of saving the testicle. Don't wait to see if the pain passes, and don't let embarrassment slow you down.

See a doctor within a day or two for pain with fever, redness, or burning when you urinate.

## Your first self-check tonight

Tonight, after a warm shower, take two minutes to go through the six steps. Find the epididymis on each side so you know what's supposed to be there. Then start your self-check note with today's date and "normal," which gives you a baseline to compare against later.
