Add it up: the drive or train ride to work, eight hours at a desk, dinner at the table, a couple of episodes on the couch. For many adults, most waking hours happen in a chair, and that's simply how a lot of modern jobs and evenings are built.

So what does that do to your health? **Research has linked sitting too much with higher blood pressure, high blood sugar, extra fat around the waist, and unhealthy cholesterol levels, and the CDC and the American Heart Association advise adults to move more and sit less throughout the day.** You don't have to quit your desk job. The answer comes down to breaking up long stretches of sitting and adding movement where you can.

Headlines sometimes claim that sitting is "the new smoking." That overstates what's known. The research shows real links, mostly from observational studies, and it points to a practical answer: get up more often.

## What research says about the health risks of sitting too much

Mayo Clinic summarizes the research this way: sitting for long periods has been linked with a cluster of health concerns, including:

- Higher blood pressure
- High blood sugar
- Extra body fat around the waist
- Unhealthy cholesterol levels
- A higher risk of death from heart disease and cancer

Several of these, like blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol, are the same numbers your doctor watches for heart health. If you're working on any of them, sitting time is one more lever you can pull. Our guide on [how to lower blood pressure](/heart-health/how-to-lower-blood-pressure/) covers the other big habits.

Most of this evidence shows an association, which means people who sit more tend to have these problems. It doesn't prove that sitting alone causes them. People who sit a lot may also move less overall, sleep differently, or eat differently. That's one reason the advice focuses on adding movement rather than fearing chairs.

## Why your workout may not cancel out a sitting-heavy day

It's common to think, "I go to the gym, so I'm covered." Regular exercise does a lot for your heart, and the CDC recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults.

But the CDC and the AHA give two separate pieces of advice: get your weekly activity, **and** sit less. Mayo Clinic notes that the impact of movement, even leisurely movement, can be significant, and that breaking up sitting through the day is part of the picture. A morning workout followed by ten unbroken hours in a chair is better than no workout, but there's still room to improve.

The AHA also points out that even light-intensity activity, like standing up and walking around, can help offset some of the risks of being sedentary. That's useful if a full workout isn't realistic on a given day.

## A day plan to break up sitting time

Mayo Clinic suggests taking a break from sitting every 30 minutes. Here's one way that can look across a typical workday. Adjust the times to your own schedule.

| Time of day | Usual sitting | Break-up move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Morning commute | Car, bus, or train | Park farther away, get off a stop early, or stand on the train if it's safe |
| First work block | Desk, email | Stand up every 30 minutes; refill water at the farthest fountain or kitchen |
| Meetings and calls | Seated meeting | Stand or pace during phone calls; suggest a walking meeting for one-on-ones |
| Lunch | Eating at the desk | Eat away from your desk, then walk for 10 minutes |
| Afternoon block | Desk, screens | Use the stairs for a bathroom break on another floor; stretch at each hour |
| Evening meal | Table, then couch | Take a short walk after dinner or clean up the kitchen standing |
| TV or screen time | Couch | Stand up, stretch, or walk around the house during each break or between episodes |

You don't have to do every row. Pick two or three that fit your day and build from there.

## Desk and TV-time swaps that add movement

Small changes count because you repeat them every day. A few easy swaps:

| Instead of... | Try... |
| --- | --- |
| Emailing a coworker down the hall | Walking over to ask in person |
| Sitting through every phone call | Standing up or pacing while you talk |
| Using the closest restroom | Using one farther away, or on another floor |
| Keeping a water bottle on your desk | Using a small glass you have to refill often |
| Scrolling on the couch | Scrolling while standing, or saving it for a walk break |
| Watching the next episode right away | Standing up and walking around until it starts |
| Sitting while you wait (kettle, microwave, kids' practice) | Standing, stretching your calves, or walking a lap |

If your workplace allows it, working at a high counter or raised surface for part of the day lets you change positions. Standing all day isn't the goal. Switching positions and moving often is.

## Sitting paths by profile

### If you work at a desk all day

Set a reminder on your computer or phone to stand every 30 minutes, and tie breaks to tasks you already do, like finishing an email or ending a call. Our [walking for heart health](/heart-health/walking-for-heart-health/) plan works well with a lunch walk.

### If you drive for a living or take long trips

Plan stops where you can get out and walk around. MedlinePlus lists sitting for long periods, such as on long trips, among the risk factors for blood clots in the legs. On flights or bus rides, get up to walk when it's allowed, and flex your ankles and move your legs while seated.

### If you're retired or spend most of the day at home

Build movement into routines: walk while on the phone, do household chores in shorter rounds, and get up during every TV break. A short walk outdoors each day adds both movement and fresh air.

### If you notice swollen ankles after long sitting

Mild swelling at the end of a long seated day is common and often eases when you move and raise your legs. Our guide to [swollen ankles and feet](/heart-health/swollen-ankles-and-feet/) explains when swelling is everyday and when it's worth a checkup.

## Leg symptoms after sitting that need care

Most aches and stiffness after sitting ease as you move. A few symptoms need medical attention:

- **Get prompt medical care** if one leg becomes swollen, painful, warm, or red, especially after a long trip or a period of not moving. MedlinePlus lists these as possible signs of deep vein thrombosis, a blood clot in a deep vein.
- **Call 911** for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain that's worse with a deep breath, a fast heartbeat, or coughing up blood. These can be signs that a clot has moved to the lungs.
- **See your doctor** if swelling in both legs doesn't go down overnight, keeps getting worse, or comes with shortness of breath.
- **See your doctor** if back, hip, or leg pain from sitting lasts for weeks or comes with numbness or weakness.
- **Check with your doctor** before starting a new exercise routine if you have heart disease or have had chest pain with activity.

## Stand up once more today

Before you finish reading, stand up and walk for two minutes, to the kitchen, the window, or the end of the hallway and back. Then set a 30-minute reminder on your phone for the rest of the day, and start the sitting log tomorrow.

As you move more, you may start noticing your heartbeat more often too. If it sometimes flutters, races, or seems to skip, our guide to [heart palpitations](/heart-health/heart-palpitations-causes/) explains common everyday triggers and when to get checked.
