It's 3 a.m., and you're up for the second time. You stand there waiting for a stream that takes its time to start, then trickles, then stops before you feel finished. Back in bed, you wonder if this is just what getting older looks like, and whether it will ever go back to the way it was.

**An enlarged prostate usually does not shrink back to its old size on its own, but that doesn't mean you're stuck with the symptoms.** Prescription medicines can relax the muscles around the prostate or slowly shrink it, and procedures can open the path urine has to take. An enlarged prostate is not cancer and is not dangerous for most men, but ignoring it can lead to problems that are much harder to live with.

Here is the honest version: what can actually change the size of your prostate, what only eases symptoms, which home remedies don't hold up, and the signs that mean you should call a doctor today.

## Why the prostate grows in the first place

The prostate is a small gland that sits just below the bladder and wraps around the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body. It makes part of the fluid in semen.

For most men, the prostate slowly gets bigger with age. Doctors call this **benign prostatic hyperplasia**, or BPH. "Benign" means it isn't cancer. "Hyperplasia" means more cells. Because the prostate surrounds the urethra, extra growth can squeeze that tube, a bit like a hand slowly tightening around a garden hose. That squeeze is what causes most of the symptoms.

Cleveland Clinic and Mayo Clinic both describe BPH as very common in older men. If it's happening to you, you're in the majority, not the exception.

## So, can it go back to its normal size?

Usually not on its own. But "shrinking" and "feeling better" are two different goals, and most men care more about the second one. Some treatments change the size of the prostate. Others leave the size alone and simply make it easier to urinate.

| Option | Does it change the size? | What it does | How fast it works |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday habits | No | Ease nighttime trips, urgency, and irritation | Days to weeks |
| Alpha blockers (prescription) | No | Relax muscles in the prostate and bladder neck so urine flows more easily | Often within days to a few weeks |
| 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (prescription, such as finasteride or dutasteride) | Yes, it can shrink over time | Lower a hormone that drives prostate growth | Slowly, over several months |
| A combination of both medicines | Yes, through the second medicine | Symptom relief now plus slower growth over time | Weeks for relief, months for size |
| Procedures and surgery | Yes, in the area that matters | Remove, shrink, or hold back the tissue that blocks the urethra | Usually the most lasting change |

One more thing surprises many men: **a bigger prostate doesn't always mean worse symptoms.** Some men with a large prostate have few problems, and some with a modest one have a lot. That's why doctors look at your symptoms, your urine flow, and the size of the prostate together before suggesting a treatment.

Every medicine on this list can have side effects. Alpha blockers, for example, can make some men dizzy when they stand up. Your doctor can explain the trade-offs for your situation.

## Is an enlarged prostate dangerous?

For most men, no. BPH is not cancer, and it is not life-threatening on its own. Mayo Clinic notes, though, that it can lead to complications when it goes untreated, including:

- **Suddenly being unable to urinate** (acute urinary retention), which needs emergency care
- **Urinary tract infections**, because urine left in the bladder gives bacteria a place to grow
- **Bladder stones**, for the same reason
- **Bladder or kidney damage** in more serious, long-lasting cases

There is also a practical reason not to wait. Cleveland Clinic points out that symptoms like a weak stream or frequent urination can also come from other conditions, including prostate cancer. A checkup is how you find out which one you're dealing with.

> **Get emergency care right away** if you can't urinate at all, especially if your lower belly feels full and painful.

## A quick check: how much is it bothering you?

Doctors often use a short questionnaire to measure urinary symptoms. This simple version helps you put a word to what you've been noticing. Think about the past month and note how often each one happens:

| Over the past month, how often did you… | Rarely | Sometimes | Most days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feel your bladder wasn't empty after urinating? | | | |
| Need to go again less than two hours later? | | | |
| Stop and start several times while urinating? | | | |
| Find it hard to wait once you felt the urge? | | | |
| Have a weak stream? | | | |
| Have to push or strain to begin? | | | |
| Get up at night to urinate (more than once)? | | | |

If you marked "most days" for any row, or if these symptoms are affecting your sleep, your work, or your plans, bring this list to your doctor. It turns a vague "things aren't great down there" into something your doctor can act on.

## Which first steps fit you

### If your symptoms are mild and fairly new

Start with the everyday habits below and a checkup. Many men with mild symptoms are simply monitored with regular visits, sometimes called watchful waiting, and only start treatment if things get worse.

### If nights are the worst part

Nighttime trips wreck sleep, and poor sleep drags down everything else. Move most of your drinking to earlier in the day, go easy on alcohol and caffeine in the evening, and empty your bladder right before bed. If you're still up several times a night, tell your doctor. Waking at night to urinate has other possible causes too, and they're worth ruling out.

### If you take cold or allergy medicines

Some cold and allergy medicines, especially decongestants and antihistamines, can make it harder to urinate, and NIDDK lists them among the medicines to watch with BPH. If your symptoms flared after a cold, check the labels and ask your pharmacist about alternatives. Don't stop any prescribed medicine on your own.

### If you also see blood, feel pain, or have a fever

Skip the home steps and see a doctor promptly. These signs can point to an infection, stones, or another problem that needs treatment.

## Everyday habits that can ease symptoms

These steps don't shrink the prostate, but NIDDK and Mayo Clinic suggest habits like these because they can make daily life noticeably easier:

- **Limit drinks a few hours before bed or before going out.** Keep drinking enough during the day.
- **Cut back on caffeine and alcohol,** which make the body produce more urine and can irritate the bladder. Our list of [common bladder irritants](/womens-health/bladder-irritants-foods-and-drinks/) applies to men too.
- **Go when you first feel the urge,** rather than holding it for a long time.
- **Try "double voiding":** urinate, wait a few moments, then try again to empty the bladder more fully.
- **Stay active.** Inactivity can make it harder to empty the bladder.
- **Keep track for a few days.** A simple bathroom diary, with times, amounts, and drinks, shows patterns quickly. Our guide to [frequent urination](/womens-health/frequent-urination-in-women/) has a three-day template you can copy.

## What about saw palmetto, nettle tea, and apple cider vinegar?

Search for "how to shrink an enlarged prostate" and you'll find long lists of natural remedies. Here is what the evidence actually shows.

- **Saw palmetto:** The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health reports that a large, high-dose study and a Cochrane review found it was no more effective than a placebo for urinary symptoms of BPH.
- **Nettle, beta-sitosterol, and other herbal products:** We did not find good-quality evidence that they shrink the prostate. Supplements are not tested by the FDA for effectiveness before they are sold, and some can interact with medicines.
- **Apple cider vinegar:** There is no good evidence that drinking it, or soaking in it, changes the size of the prostate.
- **Kegel exercises:** They don't shrink the prostate, but pelvic floor exercises may help some men with bladder control, such as dribbling after urinating.

The real risk with home remedies isn't usually the remedy itself. It's the months spent waiting for something to work while symptoms, and sometimes complications, quietly get worse. If you want to try something, tell your doctor first.

## Questions to take to your urologist

A primary care doctor can start the evaluation, and a urologist specializes in prostate problems. Bring your symptom check and your bathroom diary, and ask:

- Is this BPH, or could something else be causing my symptoms?
- How big is my prostate, and does the size matter in my case?
- Would a medicine that relaxes the prostate, one that shrinks it, or both make sense for me?
- What side effects should I watch for, including any sexual side effects?
- If medicines aren't enough, which procedures would you consider, and what is recovery like?
- Do any of my current medicines make my symptoms worse?

## Signs that need a doctor soon

Book an appointment soon if you notice any of these:

- Urinating feels harder, weaker, or more frequent than it used to
- You wake up to urinate more than once most nights
- You feel your bladder never fully empties
- You see blood in your urine
- You feel pain or burning when you urinate, or you have a fever or chills
- Symptoms started or got worse after a new medicine

And again: being unable to urinate at all is an emergency.
