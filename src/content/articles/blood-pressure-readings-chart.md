The cuff tightens, the machine beeps, and two numbers flash on the screen: 134 over 82. Is that fine? Borderline? A problem? Most people leave the pharmacy or the doctor's office with a number and no idea what it means.

Here is how to read it. **The American Heart Association (AHA) lists normal blood pressure as less than 120 for the top number and less than 80 for the bottom number.** Readings above that fall into elevated, Stage 1, or Stage 2 categories, and very high readings can be an emergency. The chart below shows each range, and the rest of this page explains what to do with your number.

A higher reading is very common, and it's something you can act on. The key is reading it correctly and not letting a single number scare or reassure you too much.

## What the top and bottom blood pressure numbers mean

Every reading is written as two numbers, like 118/76, and measured in millimeters of mercury (mm Hg).

- **Systolic (the top number)** is the pressure in your arteries when your heart beats and pushes blood out.
- **Diastolic (the bottom number)** is the pressure in your arteries while your heart rests between beats.

Both numbers matter. The AHA notes that the top number often gets more attention after about age 50, because it tends to climb as arteries stiffen with age. But a high bottom number counts too, and either one alone can put you in a higher category.

## The blood pressure chart: American Heart Association categories

These are the AHA's published categories for adults. Guidance can be updated over time, so ask your doctor which targets apply to you.

| Category | Systolic (top) |  | Diastolic (bottom) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal** | Less than 120 | and | Less than 80 |
| **Elevated** | 120 to 129 | and | Less than 80 |
| **High blood pressure, Stage 1** | 130 to 139 | or | 80 to 89 |
| **High blood pressure, Stage 2** | 140 or higher | or | 90 or higher |
| **Hypertensive crisis** (see below) | Higher than 180 | and/or | Higher than 120 |

**Read the "and" and "or" carefully.** To be "Normal," both numbers must be in range. For Stage 1 or Stage 2, only one number needs to be high. When your top and bottom numbers fall into different rows, the AHA says the higher category is the one that applies. So 126/84 counts as Stage 1, not Elevated.

## Reading your blood pressure number: a quick next-step guide

This table is a general starting point, not a diagnosis. Your doctor will weigh your age, other conditions, and repeat readings.

| If your readings are usually... | What it can mean | A sensible next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Your blood pressure is in a healthy range | Keep up your habits and get it checked at regular visits |
| Elevated | Higher than ideal, but not yet high blood pressure | Look at daily habits like sodium, activity, and sleep. Recheck over the coming months |
| Stage 1 | High blood pressure | Talk to your doctor. Many people start with lifestyle changes, and some also need medicine, depending on their overall heart risk |
| Stage 2 | High blood pressure | See your doctor soon. Treatment often includes lifestyle changes and medicine |
| Above 180/120 | Possible hypertensive crisis | Wait a minute and recheck. Follow the steps in the warning section below |

Lifestyle changes that health agencies recommend are covered in our guide on [how to lower blood pressure](/heart-health/how-to-lower-blood-pressure/).

## Why one blood pressure reading isn't a diagnosis

Blood pressure moves all day. It rises when you climb stairs, feel rushed, drink coffee, or need to use the bathroom. It dips when you rest and sleep. A single reading only shows one moment.

That's why the NHLBI explains that doctors usually diagnose high blood pressure based on **two or more readings taken on separate occasions**. Your doctor may also ask you to track your numbers at home, or to wear a monitor that measures blood pressure over 24 hours.

Two patterns are worth knowing about:

- **White coat hypertension.** Your readings are higher at the doctor's office than at home, often from nerves.
- **Masked hypertension.** The opposite: normal readings at the office, but high readings in daily life.

Home readings help sort out both. They only help, though, if you take them the right way.

## How to measure blood pressure at home, step by step

The AHA recommends a validated, automatic, upper-arm cuff monitor for home use. Wrist and finger monitors tend to be less reliable. Bring your monitor to a visit so your doctor or nurse can check that it reads correctly and the cuff is the right size.

Then follow this routine each time:

1. **Skip caffeine, exercise, and smoking for 30 minutes before.** Each can raise your reading for a short time.
2. **Empty your bladder.** A full bladder can push your numbers up.
3. **Sit in a chair with your back supported** and both feet flat on the floor. Don't cross your legs.
4. **Rest your arm on a table** so your upper arm is at about heart level.
5. **Put the cuff on bare skin**, not over a sleeve, with the bottom edge just above the bend of your elbow.
6. **Sit quietly for 5 minutes** before you start. No talking, phone, or TV.
7. **Take two or three readings, about 1 minute apart**, and write all of them down.
8. **Measure at the same times every day**, such as morning before medicines and evening before dinner, or as your doctor suggests.

### A one-week averaging sheet

Doctors often look at the average of several days rather than any one reading. Copy this and fill it in for a week:

## Paths by profile: making sense of your blood pressure readings

### If you're in your 20s or 30s

High blood pressure isn't only an older adult's condition. If you get an elevated or high reading, take it seriously and recheck it. Habits started now can shape your numbers for decades.

### If you're over 60

Your top number may be creeping up while your bottom number stays normal or even drops. That still counts, because the higher category applies. Also tell your doctor if you feel lightheaded when you stand, since some medicines and age-related changes can cause drops in pressure.

### If you have diabetes or kidney disease

These conditions raise heart and kidney risk, so your doctor may set a specific goal for you. Ask what number you're aiming for and how often to check.

## Blood pressure readings that need urgent help

If your reading is **higher than 180/120**, wait a minute and measure again. If it stays that high:

- **Call 911** if you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, changes in vision, or difficulty speaking. The AHA says this may be a hypertensive emergency with possible organ damage.
- **Contact your doctor right away** if the reading stays above 180/120 but you have none of those symptoms.

Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort spreading to the arm, jaw, or back can also be a heart attack, even with a normal reading. Know those signs too: see our guide to [heart attack warning signs](/heart-health/heart-attack-warning-signs/).

Tonight, sit down for five quiet minutes, take two readings a minute apart, and write them on day one of the sheet above. If you don't have a monitor, plan a stop at your pharmacy or doctor's office this week and note the number and time.
