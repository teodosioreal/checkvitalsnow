You wake up tired. You push through the afternoon on coffee. By evening you have nothing left for your family, your friends, or yourself. And when you mention it, people say "everyone's tired," which doesn't help.

**Feeling tired all the time is common in women, but it usually has a cause you can find.** The most frequent ones are poor sleep, iron deficiency, an underactive thyroid, ongoing stress, depression, hormonal shifts such as perimenopause, and certain medicines. Many of these can be checked with simple blood tests, and most can be improved once you know what you are dealing with.

## Tired vs. fatigued

Everyone feels tired after a bad night or a busy week. **Fatigue** is different. It is a lasting lack of energy that doesn't go away with rest and gets in the way of daily life. If that sounds like you, it is worth looking for the reason.

## 9 common causes of fatigue in women

### 1. Poor sleep

Mayo Clinic lists poor sleep habits and lack of exercise among the most common causes of fatigue. That includes going to bed late, scrolling on your phone in bed, irregular sleep times, and too much caffeine or alcohol, which MedlinePlus also mentions.

### 2. Iron deficiency

Iron helps your blood carry oxygen. When iron runs low, you may feel weak and worn out. The Office on Women's Health lists fatigue and weakness as very common symptoms of iron-deficiency anemia, along with dizziness, headaches, pale skin, and shortness of breath.

Women are more likely to have low iron than men. **Heavy menstrual periods** and pregnancy are common reasons. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements recommends 18 mg of iron a day for women ages 19 to 50.

Don't start iron pills on your own. Too much iron can be harmful, and a blood test can show whether you need it.

### 3. Thyroid problems

An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) slows your metabolism. Fatigue is a classic symptom, along with weight gain, feeling cold, dry skin, and constipation. A simple blood test checks thyroid function.

### 4. Chronic stress

Long-term stress keeps your body on alert, which can disrupt sleep and drain energy. Read our guide on [managing stress and cortisol](/womens-health/how-to-lower-cortisol-naturally/) for practical steps.

### 5. Depression and anxiety

Depression often shows up as low energy, not just sadness. Anxiety can make it hard to rest. Both are treatable, and both are worth discussing with a doctor.

### 6. Perimenopause and menopause

Hormone swings, night sweats, and broken sleep can make you exhausted during the years around menopause. Learn more about [perimenopause symptoms](/womens-health/perimenopause-symptoms/).

### 7. Sleep apnea

Sleep apnea causes your breathing to stop and start during sleep. You may not know it is happening. Loud snoring, gasping, morning headaches, and daytime sleepiness are clues.

### 8. Diabetes and other conditions

Mayo Clinic and MedlinePlus list diabetes, heart and lung disease, kidney and liver problems, and infections among medical causes of fatigue.

### 9. Medicines

Some antidepressants, antihistamines, and pain or nausea medicines can cause tiredness. Don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own, but ask your doctor whether it could be a factor.

## What you can do now

Start with the basics. They help with almost every cause:

- **Keep a regular sleep schedule**, even on weekends.
- **Put screens away** an hour before bed.
- **Limit caffeine** after midday and go easy on alcohol.
- **Move every day.** Even a short walk can boost energy over time.
- **Eat regular meals** with protein, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables. Include iron-rich foods such as lean meat, fish, eggs, beans, and iron-fortified cereals.
- **Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C** (like fruit or peppers) to help absorption, and avoid drinking coffee or tea with iron-rich meals.
- **Drink enough water.**
- **Make room for rest and stress relief.**

## When to see a doctor

Mayo Clinic suggests seeing a doctor if your fatigue hasn't improved after **two weeks** of rest, less stress, good food, and plenty of fluids. Also make an appointment if fatigue:

- Comes on suddenly or has no clear cause
- Makes it hard to work or handle daily tasks
- Comes with unexplained weight loss, fever, or night sweats
- Comes with very heavy periods

Your doctor may order blood tests to check for anemia, iron levels, thyroid function, blood sugar, and kidney and liver function.

**Get emergency care** if fatigue comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, a fast or irregular heartbeat, feeling like you might faint, confusion, severe headache, or thoughts of harming yourself.
