If you're reading this because you or someone near you has chest pain, pressure, or trouble breathing right now, **stop and call 911.** Come back to this page later.

If you're reading ahead, that's a smart move. **The main heart attack warning signs are chest pain or discomfort, discomfort in the arms, back, neck, jaw, or stomach, shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, and lightheadedness.** Signs can be sudden and intense, but many heart attacks start slowly, with mild discomfort that comes and goes. If you think it could be a heart attack, call 911, even if you're not sure.

Many people wait because they don't want to make a fuss over what might be heartburn. That hesitation is very human, and it's also the most common way precious time is lost. Emergency teams would much rather check you and find nothing than arrive too late.

## Classic heart attack warning signs to know by heart

The American Heart Association (AHA) and the CDC list these as the main signs:

- **Chest discomfort.** Most heart attacks involve discomfort in the center or left side of the chest that lasts more than a few minutes, or goes away and comes back. It can feel like uncomfortable pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain.
- **Discomfort in other areas of the upper body.** This can include pain or discomfort in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach.
- **Shortness of breath**, with or without chest discomfort.
- **Other signs**, such as breaking out in a cold sweat, nausea, or lightheadedness. The CDC also lists feeling weak or faint.

People often describe it as "not quite pain" or "a weight on my chest." It doesn't have to be dramatic or crushing to be serious.

## Heart attack symptoms in women, older adults, and people with diabetes

Chest pain or discomfort is the most common symptom for everyone. But some groups are more likely to have other symptoms, or symptoms that are easy to brush off.

### Heart attack signs in women

The AHA notes that women are somewhat more likely than men to have:

- Shortness of breath
- Nausea or vomiting
- Back or jaw pain

Because these can feel like the flu, indigestion, or stress, women sometimes wait longer to call for help. If something feels wrong and you have any of these with or without chest discomfort, call 911.

### Heart attack signs in older adults and people with diabetes

MedlinePlus explains that some people, including older adults, people with diabetes, and women, may have **little or no chest pain**. They may have unusual symptoms instead, such as shortness of breath, fatigue, or weakness. Some heart attacks are only found later, sometimes called "silent" heart attacks.

If you care for an older parent, watch for sudden, unexplained shortness of breath, weakness, or confusion, and don't wait for chest pain before calling.

## The first minutes: what to do during a heart attack

When heart muscle loses blood flow, damage builds the longer it lasts. Treatment works best when it starts quickly. Here's what to do, step by step:

1. **Call 911 right away.** Don't wait to see if it passes. Don't call your doctor's office first.
2. **Don't drive yourself**, and if possible don't have someone drive you either. The AHA explains that emergency teams can begin treatment on the way and alert the hospital before you arrive.
3. **Stop what you're doing and sit or lie down** in a position that's comfortable.
4. **Follow the dispatcher's instructions.** They may tell you to chew an aspirin. Only do this if they tell you to, and tell them if you're allergic to aspirin or have been told not to take it.
5. **Unlock the front door** and turn on a porch light if you can, so responders can get in.
6. **Have your medicine list ready**, or your plan card (below), so you can hand it to the paramedics.

### If the person collapses

If someone becomes unresponsive and isn't breathing normally, they may be in **cardiac arrest**, which is different from a heart attack. Call 911, then start **Hands-Only CPR**: push hard and fast in the center of the chest. The dispatcher can coach you through it. If an AED (automated external defibrillator) is nearby, have someone bring it and follow its voice prompts.

## Heart attack or something else? Don't sort it out alone

Chest discomfort has many causes, including heartburn, muscle strain, anxiety, and lung problems. It's natural to want to figure out which one it is. But even doctors need tests, like an ECG and blood work, to tell the difference.

| What you might tell yourself | Why it's worth calling anyway |
| --- | --- |
| "It's probably just heartburn." | Heart attack discomfort can feel like indigestion, and nausea is a known sign |
| "It went away, so I'm fine." | Heart attack discomfort can come and go |
| "I'm too young, or too healthy." | Heart attacks can happen to people who feel healthy and active |
| "I don't want to bother anyone." | 911 exists for exactly this situation |
| "It doesn't really hurt." | Many people describe pressure or fullness, not sharp pain |

## Who has a higher risk of a heart attack

Knowing your risk helps you take symptoms seriously. The NHLBI lists factors that raise the chance of a heart attack, including:

- **High blood pressure.** It often has no symptoms, so regular checks matter. Our guide to [high blood pressure symptoms](/heart-health/high-blood-pressure-symptoms/) explains why.
- **High cholesterol.** See [what your cholesterol numbers mean](/heart-health/high-cholesterol-numbers/).
- Smoking
- Diabetes
- Overweight or obesity and an unhealthy eating pattern
- Little physical activity
- Older age
- A family history of early heart disease
- A previous heart attack or other heart disease

Lowering these risks is a long game. Our guide on [how to lower blood pressure](/heart-health/how-to-lower-blood-pressure/) is a practical place to start.

In an emergency, people forget things they know well, including their own medicines. Fill this in today, keep a copy in your wallet or phone, and put one on the fridge.

### A plan for your household

Talk through these points with the people you live with:

- Everyone, including older children, knows to **call 911**, not to drive.
- Everyone knows where the plan card is.
- Someone in the home is trained in CPR, or plans to take a class.
- You know where the nearest AED is at work, the gym, or other places you spend time.

## When to call 911 and when to see a doctor soon

**Call 911 right away for:**

- Chest pain, pressure, squeezing, or fullness lasting more than a few minutes, or that comes and goes
- Chest discomfort with shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, or lightheadedness
- Sudden shortness of breath, weakness, or fainting
- Someone who collapses or stops breathing normally
- Sudden face drooping, arm weakness, or trouble speaking (possible stroke)

**Make an appointment soon** if you notice chest discomfort or unusual shortness of breath that comes on with activity and eases with rest, or if you're more tired than usual during daily tasks without a clear reason. These can be early warnings from your heart that deserve a checkup.

## Fill in your heart attack plan card tonight

Before you go to bed, fill in the plan card above, snap a photo of it on your phone, and tell one person in your household where it is. It takes ten minutes, and you'll have it ready in the moment it matters most.
