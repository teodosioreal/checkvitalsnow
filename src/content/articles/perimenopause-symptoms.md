You are in your early 40s and something feels different. Your period shows up a week early, then skips a month. You wake up at 3 a.m. drenched in sweat. Little things make you snap, and you walk into rooms forgetting why. You may not have heard the word yet, but this could be **perimenopause**.

**Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause, when your hormones, mainly estrogen, rise and fall unevenly.** It can start as early as the mid-30s or as late as the mid-50s. The most common symptoms are irregular periods, hot flashes, sleep problems, and mood changes. It is a normal life stage, not a disease, but the symptoms can be disruptive, and there are real ways to feel better.

## Perimenopause vs. menopause

These two words are often mixed up:

- **Perimenopause** is the transition. Your ovaries gradually make less estrogen, but levels swing up and down. You still have periods, even if they are irregular, and you can still get pregnant.
- **Menopause** is a single point in time: **12 months in a row without a period**. According to the Office on Women's Health, the average age of menopause in the United States is 52.
- **Postmenopause** is the time after that point.

## When does perimenopause start, and how long does it last?

Cleveland Clinic explains that perimenopause may begin as early as your mid-30s or as late as your mid-50s. Some women are in it only a short time. Others spend several years in this stage.

Smoking, family history, and some medical treatments can affect when it starts.

## Common perimenopause symptoms

Not every woman has every symptom, and intensity varies a lot. The Office on Women's Health stresses there is no single experience that everyone shares. Common symptoms include:

### Irregular periods

This is often the first sign. Periods may come more or less often, last more or fewer days, and be lighter or heavier than before.

### Hot flashes and night sweats

A sudden wave of heat in your face, neck, and chest, sometimes with sweating and a racing heart. At night, they can soak your sheets and wake you up.

### Sleep problems

Trouble falling asleep or staying asleep is common, sometimes because of night sweats, sometimes on its own.

### Mood changes

Irritability, anxiety, low mood, or mood swings. Poor sleep can make these worse.

### Vaginal dryness and bladder changes

Lower estrogen can cause dryness, discomfort during sex, and bladder symptoms such as urgency or leakage. Read more in our guide to [menopause and bladder leakage](/womens-health/menopause-and-bladder-leakage/).

### Lower sex drive

Many women notice a drop in libido during this time.

### Brain fog

Trouble concentrating or remembering things is a frequent complaint.

### Joint and muscle aches

Aches and stiffness can show up or get worse.

### Fatigue

Hormone shifts, poor sleep, and heavy periods can all leave you drained. If tiredness is your main issue, see our guide on [why you might feel tired all the time](/womens-health/why-am-i-always-tired/).

## How perimenopause affects long-term health

Lower estrogen affects more than your cycle. The Office on Women's Health notes that the years around menopause bring changes in **bone health** and **heart health**. Bone loss speeds up after menopause, which is why this is a good time to focus on [protecting your bones](/womens-health/bone-health-after-menopause/).

## What helps with perimenopause symptoms

### Lifestyle steps

- **Track your cycle and symptoms.** A simple app or notebook helps you and your doctor see patterns.
- **Keep your bedroom cool** and dress in layers to manage hot flashes.
- **Notice your triggers.** Spicy food, alcohol, caffeine, and hot drinks set off hot flashes for some women.
- **Move regularly.** Exercise helps mood, sleep, weight, and bone strength.
- **Protect your sleep** with a regular schedule and a wind-down routine.
- **Manage stress** with whatever works for you: walks, breathing exercises, time with friends.
- **Use a vaginal moisturizer or lubricant** for dryness.

### Medical treatments

The Office on Women's Health notes that medicines and other treatments can relieve symptoms. Depending on your health, your doctor may discuss:

- Hormone therapy
- Low-dose vaginal estrogen for vaginal and bladder symptoms
- Non-hormonal prescription medicines for hot flashes or mood
- Birth control pills to even out cycles in some women

Each option has benefits and risks that depend on your age and health history.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if:

- Your periods are very heavy, last much longer than usual, or come very close together
- You bleed after sex or between periods
- You have any bleeding after 12 months without a period
- Symptoms affect your sleep, work, or relationships
- You feel depressed, hopeless, or very anxious

Get help right away if you have thoughts of harming yourself.
