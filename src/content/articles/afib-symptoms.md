"Your heart rhythm looks irregular." Maybe that line popped up on your wrist during a quiet evening. Maybe a nurse paused a little too long while taking your pulse. Or maybe you've noticed your heart fluttering on the stairs and wondered what that was about.

**AFib symptoms can include a fluttering, racing, or irregular heartbeat, tiredness, shortness of breath, dizziness, weakness, and chest discomfort, but some people with atrial fibrillation feel nothing at all.** Either way, AFib deserves a doctor's attention, because it raises the risk of stroke, and that risk can be lowered with treatment.

AFib is one of the most common heart rhythm problems, according to the CDC, and it becomes more common with age. Plenty of people live full, active lives with it once it's diagnosed and managed. The hard part is often just getting it noticed.

## What happens in the heart during atrial fibrillation

A healthy heartbeat starts with a steady electrical signal in the upper chambers (the atria). That signal tells the atria to squeeze, then passes to the lower chambers (the ventricles), which pump blood to the body.

In AFib, the electrical signals in the atria become fast and disorganized. Instead of squeezing firmly, the atria quiver. The ventricles get a jumble of signals and beat irregularly, often faster than normal. NHLBI explains that AFib can come and go, or it can last a long time or become permanent.

## AFib symptoms people notice most

The American Heart Association and NHLBI describe a similar set of symptoms. You might have one, several, or none:

- **A fluttering, quivering, or "flip-flopping" heartbeat**
- **A racing or pounding heartbeat** that feels irregular rather than steady
- **Tiredness** or feeling worn out with less activity than usual
- **Shortness of breath**, especially with activity or lying down
- **Dizziness or lightheadedness**
- **Chest pain or pressure**
- **Weakness** or trouble exercising

Some of these overlap with ordinary palpitations. Our guide to [heart palpitations causes](/heart-health/heart-palpitations-causes/) covers the many harmless triggers too. The clue that points more toward AFib is a rhythm that feels **irregular**, with no steady pattern, rather than just fast.

### "Silent" AFib with no symptoms

Some people never feel AFib. It shows up on an ECG done for another reason, during a routine pulse check, or after a device alert. No symptoms doesn't mean no risk: silent AFib can still lead to clots and stroke.

## Why AFib raises stroke risk

When the atria quiver instead of squeezing, blood can pool and move slowly in part of the heart. Pooled blood is more likely to form clots. If a clot breaks loose and travels to the brain, it can block an artery and cause a stroke. The CDC and NHLBI both describe AFib as a major risk factor for stroke.

That's why treatment usually aims at two things: controlling the heart rate or rhythm, and lowering the risk of clots, often with blood-thinning medicine. Your doctor weighs your other risks, such as age, high blood pressure, diabetes, and past strokes, to decide what's right for you.

Learn the stroke warning signs so you'd spot them in yourself or someone close to you. Our guide to [signs of a stroke](/heart-health/stroke-signs/) includes a wallet card you can copy.

## What raises the chance of AFib

NHLBI and the CDC list these risk factors:

| Risk factor | What you can do about it |
| --- | --- |
| Older age | Can't change it, but it's a reason to check your pulse and attend regular checkups |
| High blood pressure | Get it measured and treated; see our guide to [high blood pressure symptoms](/heart-health/high-blood-pressure-symptoms/) |
| Heart disease or heart valve problems | Keep up with your heart care and follow-up visits |
| Obesity | Talk to your doctor about a realistic plan for weight and activity |
| Diabetes | Work with your care team on blood sugar control |
| Heavy alcohol use | Cutting back may help; ask your doctor what's safe for you |
| Sleep apnea | Mention loud snoring or pauses in breathing at night; it can be tested and treated |
| Thyroid problems | A blood test can check your thyroid |

## Smartwatch AFib alerts: what they can and can't tell you

Some smartwatches and fitness devices can check your pulse in the background and flag an irregular rhythm that may be AFib. Some can also record a short single-lead ECG when you touch the device.

These alerts can be a useful nudge, but keep them in perspective:

- **A device alert is not a diagnosis.** A doctor needs to confirm AFib, usually with an electrocardiogram (ECG) or a heart monitor.
- **A normal reading doesn't rule AFib out.** If you have symptoms, see a doctor even if your device shows nothing.
- **Save the alert.** Note the date and time, take a screenshot, and bring it to your appointment.
- **Don't change medicines** based on a device reading. Talk to your doctor first.

If an alert comes with chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or stroke signs, call 911 rather than waiting for an appointment.

## How doctors confirm AFib

MedlinePlus and NHLBI describe the usual steps:

1. **History and exam.** Your doctor asks about your symptoms and listens to your heart.
2. **Electrocardiogram (ECG).** The main test for AFib. It records your heart's electrical activity for a few minutes.
3. **Heart monitor.** If AFib comes and goes, you may wear a portable monitor for a day or longer.
4. **Echocardiogram.** An ultrasound shows the heart's size, pumping, and valves.
5. **Blood tests**, often including thyroid tests.

Treatment may include medicines to control rate or rhythm, blood thinners, a procedure to reset the rhythm (cardioversion), or a procedure called catheter ablation. Lifestyle changes, such as managing blood pressure, limiting alcohol, and treating sleep apnea, are part of the plan too.

## AFib paths by situation

### If you just got a device alert and feel fine

Book a visit with your primary care doctor. Bring the saved alert and note any symptoms you may have brushed off, like getting winded more easily.

### If you've felt an irregular flutter that comes and goes

Write down when it happens, how long it lasts, and what you were doing. Episodes that come and go are easier to catch with a monitor if your doctor knows the pattern.

### If you've already been diagnosed

Keep taking your medicines as prescribed and ask before stopping any of them. Know your stroke signs, and tell every new doctor or dentist that you have AFib and which medicines you take.

## Questions to ask your doctor about AFib

Copy these into your phone and bring them to your appointment:

- Do I have AFib, or could something else explain my symptoms?
- Does my AFib come and go, or is it constant?
- What is my stroke risk, and do I need a blood thinner?
- What are the side effects of my medicines, and what should I watch for?
- Would a procedure such as cardioversion or ablation make sense for me?
- Could sleep apnea, alcohol, thyroid problems, or blood pressure be playing a role?
- Is it safe for me to exercise, and how hard?
- Should I keep using my smartwatch alerts, and what should I do when one goes off?
- What symptoms mean I should call 911?

## When AFib symptoms need 911

**Call 911** for:

- Chest pain or pressure
- Fainting or nearly fainting
- Severe shortness of breath
- Sudden face drooping, arm or leg weakness, trouble speaking, vision loss, or loss of balance (signs of stroke)

**See a doctor soon** if you notice a new irregular heartbeat, an AFib alert from a device, episodes that are longer or more frequent, or new tiredness and breathlessness.

## Check your pulse rhythm today

Sit quietly for five minutes, then find your pulse on your wrist and feel it for a full minute. Is it steady, or does it skip around with no pattern? Write down what you notice. If it feels irregular, call your doctor's office this week and describe what you found.
