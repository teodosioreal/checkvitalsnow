Most lab results come with a neat reference range. PSA is different. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) states plainly that **there is no specific normal or abnormal level of PSA in the blood**, which surprises a lot of men staring at a number on a patient portal.

**PSA test results show how much prostate-specific antigen is in your blood, but no single cutoff separates "normal" from "cancer." A higher PSA can come from prostate cancer, and it can just as easily come from an enlarged prostate, prostatitis, or other causes. Your doctor reads the number together with your age, risk, past results, and sometimes more tests.**

Feeling uneasy about a PSA result is normal. A number that is higher than expected is a reason for a conversation, not a reason to panic. Most of what happens next is careful, step-by-step checking.

## What PSA is, in one paragraph

Prostate-specific antigen is a protein made by cells in the prostate, the gland below the bladder. Small amounts end up in the blood. MedlinePlus explains that the PSA test is a blood draw that measures this protein. Both normal and cancerous prostate cells make PSA, which is exactly why the test can't tell them apart on its own.

## Why a "normal PSA" number doesn't exist

NCI notes that doctors used to treat a PSA of 4.0 ng/mL or lower as normal. Later research changed that view. Some men with PSA below 4.0 have prostate cancer, and many men with higher levels don't. So today, doctors don't rely on one line. They consider factors such as:

- **Your age,** since PSA tends to rise as the prostate grows with age
- **Your past results,** because a change over time can say more than a single value
- **The size of your prostate,** if known
- **Your personal risk,** such as family history of prostate cancer and being Black, which the USPSTF identifies as factors linked to higher risk
- **Your medicines,** because some can lower PSA (see below)

If you've read charts of "PSA levels by age" online, bring them as questions rather than answers. Your doctor's interpretation of your own trend matters more.

## Things besides cancer that can change a PSA result

NCI lists several things that can raise PSA. Others can push it down. Knowing these helps explain a surprising number and is why a high result is often retested.

| Can raise PSA | Can lower PSA |
| --- | --- |
| An enlarged prostate (BPH). Our guide to [whether an enlarged prostate can go back to normal](/mens-health/enlarged-prostate-back-to-normal/) explains the condition | Some medicines for BPH, such as finasteride and dutasteride, which NCI notes can lower PSA |
| Prostatitis, an inflammation or infection of the prostate. See [prostatitis vs. an enlarged prostate](/mens-health/prostatitis-vs-enlarged-prostate/) | Tell your doctor about every prescription, and anything you take without one |
| Certain medical procedures on the prostate or bladder | |
| Increasing age | |

### Ask about these before the test

Labs and doctors sometimes give instructions about what to avoid in the day or two before a PSA test, such as ejaculation or long, vigorous exercise like cycling. Instructions vary, so ask your doctor or the lab what applies to you rather than guessing.

## Who should consider PSA screening: the USPSTF view

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) makes recommendations for people without symptoms. In its prostate cancer screening recommendation:

- **Men 55 to 69:** the decision to have PSA screening should be an individual one, made with a doctor after talking about benefits and harms.
- **Men 70 and older:** the USPSTF recommends against routine PSA-based screening.

The USPSTF explains that screening may lower the chance of dying from prostate cancer for some men, but it also brings possible harms, including false-positive results, biopsies with complications, and overdiagnosis of cancers that would never have caused problems. Treatment of those cancers can lead to side effects such as urinary leaking and erection problems.

Recommendations are reviewed over time, so check the USPSTF page linked in our sources for the latest version. If you have symptoms, such as trouble urinating or blood in your urine, that is not screening. See your doctor about the symptoms directly.

### Paths by situation

**If you're 55 to 69 and have never talked about it:** Book a visit to discuss whether screening fits your values and risk. There isn't a wrong answer, only an informed or uninformed one.

**If you have a father or brother with prostate cancer, or you are Black:** Ask your doctor whether to start the conversation earlier. Our guide to [men's health screenings by age](/mens-health/mens-health-screenings-by-age/) puts PSA next to the other checks for your age.

**If you're 70 or older:** Routine screening generally isn't recommended, but symptoms still deserve a checkup.

**If you take finasteride or dutasteride:** Make sure whoever reads your PSA knows, since these medicines can lower it.

## Questions to ask before and after your PSA test

Print this or save it to your phone.

**Before the test**

- What might I gain from screening, and what are the possible downsides for someone like me?
- Do my family history or other risk factors change your advice?
- Should I avoid ejaculation, cycling, or hard exercise beforehand?
- Do any of my medicines affect PSA?
- If the result is high, what would the next step be?

**After the test**

- How does this result compare with my previous ones?
- Could an enlarged prostate, an infection, or something else explain it?
- Should we repeat the test, and when?
- Are other tests, such as imaging or additional blood tests, worth doing before considering a biopsy?
- If you suggest a biopsy, what are its risks and what would we learn?

## How a high PSA is usually followed up

NCI describes several options doctors use after an elevated PSA. Not everyone needs all of them, and many men stop after the first step.

1. **Repeat the PSA test,** sometimes after treating an infection or waiting a few weeks.
2. **Look at the trend** in your results over time.
3. **Use additional tests,** such as other blood or urine tests or an MRI of the prostate, to help decide whether a biopsy makes sense.
4. **Biopsy,** in which small tissue samples are checked for cancer, when the other information points that way.

## Prostate symptoms that shouldn't wait for a screening decision

PSA screening is for men without symptoms. See a doctor promptly if you notice:

- Blood in your urine or semen
- Trouble urinating, a weak stream, or waking often at night to pee. Our guide to [peeing at night](/mens-health/peeing-at-night-men/) covers the many causes
- Pain in your lower back, hips, or pelvis that doesn't go away
- Painful urination with fever or chills

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you can't urinate at all.

## Gather your PSA history this week

Find your past PSA results, if you've had any, and write them in a simple list with dates. Then add the questions above that matter most to you and bring both to your next appointment. A trend and a clear set of questions turn a confusing number into a real decision.
