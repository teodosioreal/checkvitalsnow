Your to-do list never ends. You lie in bed with your mind racing, wake up already tense, and get through the day on adrenaline and coffee. You have seen "high cortisol" all over social media and wonder if that is what is happening to you.

**Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone, and the most reliable ways to keep it in a healthy range are everyday habits: steady sleep, regular exercise, relaxation practices, less caffeine, and strong social connections.** There is no single food, drink, or trick that "flushes" cortisol. But the right routine helps your body recover from stress instead of staying stuck in alarm mode.

## What cortisol does in your body

Cortisol is a steroid hormone made by your adrenal glands, which sit on top of your kidneys. According to Cleveland Clinic, it does much more than handle stress. Cortisol helps:

- Control how your body uses sugar (glucose) for energy
- Regulate blood pressure
- Reduce inflammation
- Manage your sleep-wake cycle

Cortisol naturally rises in the morning to help you wake up and drops at night so you can sleep.

## When stress doesn't switch off

When you face a threat or pressure, your brain signals your adrenal glands to release cortisol. Your heart beats faster, your blood sugar rises, and your body gets ready to act. Once the stress passes, levels should come back down.

The problem is **chronic stress**: work pressure, caregiving, money worries, or poor sleep that never let up. A research review in PubMed Central describes how long-term stress and high cortisol may affect parts of the brain involved in memory and thinking. Chronic stress is also linked to poor sleep, irritability, and low energy.

## Signs your stress may be too high

- Trouble falling or staying asleep
- Feeling "tired but wired"
- Irritability or feeling on edge
- Trouble focusing
- Headaches or muscle tension
- Changes in appetite
- Getting sick more often

These signs are common with chronic stress, but they also overlap with other conditions, including [hormonal imbalances](/womens-health/signs-of-hormonal-imbalance-in-women/) and [causes of fatigue](/womens-health/why-am-i-always-tired/) such as low iron or thyroid problems.

## Eight habits that help your body handle stress

The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) and the CDC recommend practical steps that are simple, free, and well supported.

### 1. Protect your sleep

The CDC notes that adults need **7 or more hours** of sleep a night and recommends going to bed and waking up at the same times each day. NIMH adds that the blue light from screens can make it harder to fall asleep, so dim or put away devices before bed.

### 2. Move your body

The CDC suggests working up to about **2½ hours of activity a week**, which you can break into 20 to 30 minutes a day. NIMH notes that small amounts of exercise add up. Walking counts.

### 3. Breathe slowly

Slow, deep breathing calms your body's stress response. Try breathing in through your nose for a count of four, then out slowly for a count of six. Repeat for a few minutes.

### 4. Try relaxation practices

Stretching, meditation, yoga, or a relaxation app can help. NIMH lists deep breathing, stretching, and meditation among its tips.

### 5. Cut back on caffeine

NIMH advises avoiding too much caffeine from coffee, energy drinks, or soft drinks. Caffeine can make you feel more on edge and interfere with sleep.

### 6. Stay connected

Spending time with people you trust is one of the most protective things you can do. Talk to a friend, call family, or join a group.

### 7. Limit news and social media

NIMH notes that constant exposure to upsetting news can add to stress. Set limits on how often you check.

### 8. Write it down

Journaling and naming worried thoughts can help you see patterns and challenge unhelpful thinking, another tip from NIMH.

## What about "cortisol detox" drinks?

Social media is full of drinks and routines promising to lower cortisol fast. Most of these claims are not backed by solid research. The basics above are less flashy, but they are what health agencies actually recommend.

## When stress needs more than self-care

Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if:

- Stress or anxiety doesn't go away or is getting worse
- You have trouble getting through daily tasks
- You feel hopeless, or you are using alcohol or other substances to cope
- You have physical symptoms that worry you, such as rapid weight gain in the face or belly, easy bruising, or muscle weakness, which may point to a hormone disorder that needs testing

**Get help right away** if you have thoughts of harming yourself. In the U.S., you can call or text **988** to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

## Start with one habit this week

Trying to change everything at once is its own source of stress. Pick one habit from the list, such as a fixed bedtime or a ten-minute walk after lunch, and keep it for a week before adding another. Small, steady changes are easier to keep.
