You used to bounce back after a hard week. Now a trip to the grocery store can wipe you out for the rest of the day, and a busy Saturday costs you Sunday and Monday too. You sleep eight or nine hours and wake up feeling like you didn't sleep at all. Friends say they're tired too, and you want to shake them: not like this.

**Everyday tiredness gets better with rest. Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) does not. Its hallmark is that symptoms get worse after physical or mental effort, often a day or more later, and the exhaustion lasts six months or longer.** Many other conditions cause long-lasting fatigue, so testing comes first. But if your pattern matches, knowing the name and learning to pace your energy can change how you manage every week.

## Tiredness and ME/CFS are not the same thing

Everyone gets tired. ME/CFS is a serious, long-term illness that can affect many body systems. Here is how the two usually differ:

| | Everyday tiredness | ME/CFS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rest and sleep | Usually help | Don't restore energy; sleep often feels unrefreshing |
| After exercise or a busy day | Tired, then recover | Symptoms get worse, often 12 to 48 hours later, and can last days or longer |
| How long it lasts | Days to a couple of weeks | Six months or more |
| Effect on daily life | Annoying but manageable | Greatly reduces ability to work, study, or do usual activities |
| Thinking | Mildly slower when tired | Often marked trouble with memory and focus |

## The CDC's core symptoms

The CDC describes three core symptoms that people with ME/CFS have:

1. **A greatly reduced ability to do activities** that were usual before the illness, lasting six months or more, along with fatigue that is often profound, not from unusually hard effort, and not relieved by rest
2. **Post-exertional malaise (PEM):** symptoms get worse after physical or mental activity that would not have caused a problem before
3. **Unrefreshing sleep**

Plus at least one of these:

- **Problems with thinking and memory**, sometimes called brain fog
- **Orthostatic intolerance:** symptoms get worse when standing or sitting upright, such as lightheadedness, dizziness, or feeling faint

Many people also have muscle or joint pain, headaches, sore throat, or tender lymph nodes.

## A quick self-check to bring to your doctor

Answer each question honestly. This isn't a diagnosis, but it helps you describe your pattern clearly:

- [ ] I've had fatigue that limits my usual activities for six months or longer
- [ ] Rest and sleep don't make me feel restored
- [ ] After a busy day or exercise, I feel much worse, sometimes a day or two later
- [ ] That "crash" can last days or longer
- [ ] I have trouble with memory, focus, or finding words
- [ ] I feel worse, dizzy, or faint when standing for a while
- [ ] This started after an infection, such as a virus or COVID-19

If you checked the first three, mention ME/CFS specifically when you see your doctor.

## Rule out other causes first

Because so many conditions cause fatigue, MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic note that doctors diagnose ME/CFS partly by ruling others out. Expect your doctor to ask about sleep, mood, and medicines, and to consider tests such as:

- A complete blood count, to look for anemia
- Thyroid function
- Blood sugar
- Kidney and liver function
- A sleep evaluation if you snore loudly or wake gasping

Depression, sleep apnea, thyroid problems, and anemia can all cause deep fatigue, and they are treatable.

## Pacing: working with your energy

There is no cure for ME/CFS yet, but managing activity can help reduce crashes. The CDC describes pacing as a key strategy: planning activity and rest to stay within your energy limits. In practice:

- **Find your baseline.** Track how much activity you can do on a typical day without a crash afterward.
- **Stay a little under it**, even on good days. Overdoing it on a good day is the most common path to a bad week.
- **Break tasks into pieces** with rest in between, and sit down for tasks you'd usually stand for.
- **Count mental effort too.** Long calls, screens, and paperwork can trigger a crash just like physical activity.

### An energy diary to find your limits

| Date | Main activities | Energy that evening (0–10) | Energy next day (0–10) | Crash? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 2 | Groceries, 20-minute walk, 2 hours of computer work | 4 | 2 | Yes, rest of day |
| | | | | |

After two or three weeks, the pattern between what you did and how you felt a day or two later usually becomes clear.

**One caution:** pushing through with steadily increasing exercise can make post-exertional malaise worse. Talk with a doctor who understands ME/CFS before starting an exercise program.

## Where your fatigue story begins

### If it started after COVID-19 or another infection

Tell your doctor when it started. The CDC notes that fatigue and post-exertional malaise are common in long COVID, and some people meet ME/CFS criteria.

### If you also get dizzy when you stand up

That's worth mentioning, since it can be part of the picture. Our guide on [why you get dizzy when standing up](/brain-and-balance/dizzy-when-standing-up/) covers causes and simple steps.

### If you've been tired for weeks, not months

## When to get help now

See a doctor if fatigue lasts more than a few weeks, keeps you from usual activities, or comes with fever, weight loss, night sweats, or shortness of breath. **Get help right away** if you have thoughts of harming yourself; in the U.S., call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

Start the energy diary today and fill it in every evening for two weeks. Pick one task this week to split in half with a rest in between. Then bring the diary and the self-check to your doctor. Those two pages can turn a vague "I'm always tired" into a clear conversation about what's going on.
