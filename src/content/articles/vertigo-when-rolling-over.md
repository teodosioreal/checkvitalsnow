Why would the room spin only when you turn onto your left side, and never when you're walking around all day? It feels backward. Surely a balance problem would show up when you're actually on your feet.

**Vertigo when rolling over in bed is most often benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), an inner ear problem in which tiny calcium crystals drift into a balance canal and trigger brief spinning whenever you move your head in certain ways.** It's one of the most common causes of vertigo, it isn't dangerous on its own, and it's often treatable in a single office visit.

The spinning can be frightening, especially the first time, at 2 a.m., in the dark. Understanding what's going on, and what doesn't fit the pattern, makes it much less scary.

## Why lying down sets off BPPV

Your inner ear holds two kinds of balance sensors. Small organs called the utricle and saccule contain tiny calcium carbonate crystals that help sense gravity. Nearby, three fluid-filled loops called semicircular canals sense your head turning.

MedlinePlus explains that BPPV happens when some of those crystals break loose and float into one of the canals. They don't belong there. When you tip your head back onto a pillow or roll to one side, the crystals tumble through the fluid and keep moving after your head has stopped. The canal tells your brain you're still turning. Your eyes and body say you're lying still. That mismatch is the spinning.

This is why BPPV is so position-specific:

- **Rolling over, lying down, or sitting up** in bed are common triggers, as Mayo Clinic notes.
- **Looking up** to a high shelf or tilting your head back at the hair salon or dentist can do it too.
- **Walking around** usually doesn't, because your head stays fairly upright.

The word "benign" means it isn't a sign of a dangerous condition. "Paroxysmal" means it comes in sudden bursts.

### What sets off BPPV in the first place

Often, no clear cause is found. Mayo Clinic lists a few known links, such as a blow to the head and other inner ear disorders. It also becomes more common as people get older.

## Does your spinning fit BPPV? A pattern check

BPPV has a recognizable fingerprint. Compare your spells with this table, then bring it to your doctor.

| Feature | Fits BPPV | Doesn't fit, needs a closer look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What starts it | A specific head movement: rolling over, lying down, sitting up, looking up | Comes on at rest, out of nowhere, or never changes with position |
| How long the spinning lasts | Brief, often under a minute | Hours to days, or constant |
| Hearing | No change | Muffled hearing, hearing loss, ringing, or fullness in one ear |
| Head | No headache | A sudden, severe headache |
| Rest of the body | Normal strength, speech, and vision once the spinning stops | Weakness, numbness, slurred speech, double vision, facial droop |
| Walking | Unsteady for a while, but able to walk | Can't walk or stand even when your head is still |
| Stomach | Nausea possible during spells | Repeated vomiting that won't stop |

If everything lands in the middle column, BPPV is a strong possibility. If anything lands in the right column, see the warning signs section below before doing anything else.

If your dizziness feels more like faintness than spinning, especially when standing, that's a different problem. ## How doctors diagnose BPPV

There's usually no need for a scan. MedlinePlus describes the main test as a positional exam, often called the Dix-Hallpike maneuver.

Here's what to expect:

1. **Your history.** The doctor asks what triggers the spinning, how long it lasts, and whether you have hearing changes or other symptoms. Bring your pattern check.
2. **The positional test.** You sit on the exam table. The doctor turns your head to one side and helps you lie back quickly, so your head tips slightly below the table edge.
3. **Watching your eyes.** If BPPV is the cause, the spinning may start, and your eyes make a telltale jerking movement (nystagmus). The direction of that movement helps the doctor tell which ear and which canal are involved.
4. **Ruling out other causes.** A quick check of your nerves, coordination, and hearing helps confirm that nothing else is going on. If something doesn't fit, you may need hearing tests, balance tests, or imaging.

The test can bring on a spell, which is unpleasant but brief. It's also what makes the diagnosis clear.

## How BPPV is treated: repositioning maneuvers

The main treatment is a canalith repositioning procedure. The best-known version is the Epley maneuver. Mayo Clinic describes it as a series of slow head movements, each held for a short time, that use gravity to guide the loose crystals out of the canal and back to a part of the inner ear where they no longer cause trouble.

A few things are worth knowing:

- **A clinician does it first.** A doctor, audiologist, or physical therapist trained in vestibular (balance) problems usually performs it. Different canals need different maneuvers, and the side matters.
- **It may need repeating.** Some people need more than one session before the spinning stops.
- **You may feel off for a few days.** Some people feel a bit unsteady or queasy afterward. Your clinician may give you instructions for the rest of the day.
- **Ask about a home version.** Once your doctor knows which ear and canal are involved, they may teach you how to repeat the maneuver if BPPV returns.
- **Medicines play a small role.** MedlinePlus notes that medicines are sometimes used to ease nausea or spinning, but they don't work as well as repositioning to treat BPPV itself.
- **Surgery is rare.** It's an option only for the small number of people whose BPPV doesn't respond to other treatment.

We don't give step-by-step maneuver instructions here on purpose. Doing it on the wrong side, or for something that isn't BPPV, may not help and can delay the right care.

### Will BPPV come back?

It can. MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic both note that BPPV may return, even after successful treatment. Recognizing the pattern early, and knowing who to call, makes a repeat episode much less disruptive.

## Making nights safer while you have BPPV

The real risk with BPPV isn't the spinning itself. It's falling during or right after a spell, especially on the way to the bathroom at night. MedlinePlus lists dizziness and balance problems among the things that raise fall risk.

**Bedside safety checklist**

- [ ] A lamp or night-light you can reach without turning your head sharply
- [ ] A clear path from bed to bathroom, with loose rugs and cords removed
- [ ] Your phone within reach of the bed
- [ ] Get up in stages: roll slowly, sit on the edge of the bed, wait until the spinning stops, then stand
- [ ] Hold on to something steady for your first few steps
- [ ] A glass of water by the bed, so you don't need to get up for a drink
- [ ] Don't drive, climb ladders, or use power tools until spells have stopped

## BPPV paths by situation

### If you're older or live alone

Falls are the bigger danger here. Use the checklist above, and consider asking a family member to check in while you're waiting to be seen. Mention any recent falls to your doctor.

### If it started after a head injury

See a doctor before assuming it's simple BPPV. A head injury can cause other problems that need to be ruled out.

### If you've had BPPV before

If the same familiar pattern returns, contact the clinician who treated you. If they taught you a home maneuver, ask whether it's right to use again. If anything feels different from last time, get checked.

### If you also have ringing or muffled hearing in one ear

That doesn't fit typical BPPV. An inner ear condition such as Ménière's disease or inner ear inflammation is possible, and NIDCD lists both among balance disorders. See a doctor, and if hearing dropped suddenly, go promptly.

## When vertigo in bed isn't BPPV: warning signs

BPPV is common, but other causes can look similar at first. Get medical care soon if:

- Spinning **lasts hours or days**, or doesn't change with head position
- You have **hearing loss, ringing, or fullness** in one ear along with the vertigo
- Spells keep coming back despite treatment
- It started after a **head injury**

**Call 911** if vertigo or dizziness comes with any of these, which can be signs of a stroke or another emergency:

- A sudden, severe headache
- Weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg, or a drooping face
- Trouble speaking, slurred speech, or confusion
- Double vision or sudden vision loss
- Trouble walking or standing, even when your head is still
- Fainting, chest pain, or a racing or irregular heartbeat
- Vomiting that won't stop

## Before your next night: a small plan

Tonight, set up the bedside checklist: the light, the clear path, the phone. Then write down your pattern check answers while they're fresh, especially which side you were turning toward when the room spun. That single detail can help your doctor pick the right ear and the right maneuver.
