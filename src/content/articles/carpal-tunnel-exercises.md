Shaking your hands out at 3 a.m. gets old fast. So does dropping your coffee cup, or putting down your phone mid-scroll because your fingers buzz. If you've figured out it's probably carpal tunnel, the next question is what you can actually do about it, ideally before anyone mentions surgery.

**For mild to moderate carpal tunnel, the first steps are simple: keep your wrist straight at night with a splint, take pressure off the wrist during the day, and add gentle exercises if they feel good. If symptoms last or get worse, doctors can offer injections or surgery, and earlier treatment tends to protect the nerve.** Here's a plan you can start today, with what to expect at each step.

## How the treatments line up

Most people move through these steps in order, stopping when symptoms settle. Mayo Clinic and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describe the main options:

| Step | What it is | Who it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Night splint | A brace that keeps the wrist straight while you sleep | Almost everyone with symptoms, especially at night |
| Activity changes | Breaks, lighter grip, neutral wrist position | Anyone whose work or hobbies bother the wrist |
| Gentle exercises | Stretches and gliding exercises, often taught by a hand therapist | As an add-on, if they don't increase symptoms |
| Anti-inflammatory medicine | Short-term over-the-counter pain relievers | For pain relief; they don't fix the pressure on the nerve |
| Corticosteroid injection | A shot into the carpal tunnel to reduce swelling | When symptoms continue despite the steps above |
| Surgery | Cutting the tight ligament to make more room for the nerve | Severe or lasting symptoms, or signs of nerve damage |

If you're not yet sure the problem is in your wrist, start with our guide on [why hands go numb at night](/pain-and-nerves/hands-numb-at-night/), which shows how to tell which nerve is involved.

## Your night splint routine

Splinting tends to help most when symptoms wake you up.

- **Choose a splint that keeps your wrist straight**, not bent up or down. Many pharmacy splints have a metal or plastic insert for this.
- **Wear it every night** for a few weeks. Daytime use is optional; some people find it helpful during activities that trigger symptoms.
- **It should feel snug, not tight.** If your fingers swell or change color, loosen it.
- **Give it time.** Many people notice fewer night wake-ups within a few weeks.

## Gentle exercises to try

Hand therapists often teach these. Do them slowly, two or three times a day. **Stop any exercise that increases tingling or causes pain.** Research on exercises for carpal tunnel is mixed, so use them alongside the splint, not instead of it.

### Wrist stretch

Hold your arm out in front of you, palm up. With your other hand, gently bend your fingers and wrist down toward the floor until you feel a mild stretch in your forearm. Hold 15 to 20 seconds. Repeat 3 times on each hand.

### Tendon glides

Start with fingers straight. Then move through four positions, holding each for 3 seconds: a hook fist (fingertips touching the top of the palm), a full fist, a "tabletop" (knuckles bent at 90 degrees, fingers straight), and a straight fist (fingertips touching the base of the palm). Repeat 5 times.

### Median nerve glide

Make a fist with your palm facing you. Slowly straighten your fingers and thumb, then gently tilt your wrist back. Move only to the edge of comfort, hold 3 to 5 seconds, then return. Repeat 5 times. This should feel like a gentle pull, never tingling or pain.

## A 7-day hand-friendly plan

| Day | Add this | Keep doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Start wearing a night splint | |
| 2 | Raise or lower your keyboard so your wrists stay straight, as OSHA suggests | Splint |
| 3 | Set a reminder for a 1-minute hand break every hour | Splint, keyboard |
| 4 | Try the wrist stretch and tendon glides twice today | All of the above |
| 5 | Loosen your grip on your phone, steering wheel, and tools | All of the above |
| 6 | Add the median nerve glide if the others felt fine | All of the above |
| 7 | Rate your symptoms 0–10 and compare with Day 1 | Everything that helped |

If your score hasn't moved after a few weeks, it's time for an appointment.

## Daily changes that ease the pressure

- **Keep wrists straight and relaxed** when typing. OSHA's workstation guidance places the keyboard so your forearms are about parallel to the floor and your wrists stay straight.
- **Use a lighter grip** on pens, tools, phones, and steering wheels.
- **Switch hands** for tasks when you can.
- **Keep your hands warm.** Cold can make pain and stiffness worse.
- **Notice your phone posture.** Holding a phone with a bent wrist for long stretches adds up, and so does the tech-neck posture covered in our guide on [tech neck](/pain-and-nerves/tech-neck-posture/).

## When to see a doctor or consider other treatment

See a doctor if:

- Symptoms continue after a few weeks of splinting and daily changes
- Numbness is constant, not just at night
- You're weak, clumsy, or drop things
- The muscles at the base of your thumb look smaller

Mayo Clinic notes that doctors may suggest a corticosteroid injection when other measures aren't enough, and surgery when symptoms are severe or don't respond to other treatments. Waiting too long with ongoing nerve pressure can lead to lasting damage, so don't push through months of constant numbness.

**Call 911** for sudden numbness or weakness in one arm or one side of the body, especially with face drooping or trouble speaking.
