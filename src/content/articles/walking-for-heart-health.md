Myth: exercise only "counts" for your heart if you sweat through a gym class or run a few miles. Plenty of people skip activity altogether because that bar feels too high.

The real bar is lower, and it fits on a sidewalk. **For heart health, the CDC and the American Heart Association recommend that adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, and brisk walking is one of the simplest ways to get there.** That works out to about 30 minutes on five days, and it can be split into shorter walks. If you're starting from a mostly seated routine, that's very common, and ten minutes a day is a fine place to begin.

Below you'll find how to tell if your pace is brisk enough, a six-week plan that builds from 10 minutes a day, and practical tips on shoes, weather, and safety.

## Why walking for heart health is worth your time

The CDC lists a wide range of benefits from regular physical activity, including a lower risk of heart disease and stroke, better sleep, and help with weight management. Mayo Clinic notes that regular brisk walking can help you maintain a healthy weight and may help prevent or manage conditions such as heart disease, high blood pressure, and type 2 diabetes.

Walking has a few things going for it that other workouts don't:

- **No skill or equipment needed** beyond comfortable shoes.
- **Low impact**, so it's easier on most joints than running or jumping.
- **Easy to fit in**: before work, at lunch, after dinner, on the phone.
- **Easy to scale**: you can make it harder with pace, hills, or time.

The American Heart Association also stresses that some activity is better than none. If 150 minutes sounds far away, moving more than you do now is still a step in the right direction.

## The weekly walking target, in plain numbers

Here's how the CDC and AHA guidelines for adults translate into walking:

| Guideline | What it means for walking |
| --- | --- |
| At least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week | Brisk walking for 30 minutes, 5 days a week, or any mix that totals 150 |
| Activity can be spread across the week | Three 10-minute walks in a day count the same as one 30-minute walk |
| Muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days a week | Walking alone doesn't cover this. Add simple moves like squats to a chair or wall push-ups |
| Sit less, move more | Short walking breaks during long sitting stretches help too |

The AHA adds that going beyond 150 minutes, toward 300 minutes a week, brings even more benefit. Treat that as a later goal, not a starting line.

## The talk test: is your walk brisk enough?

You don't need a heart rate monitor to find a moderate pace. The CDC describes a simple check called the talk test:

- **Moderate intensity:** you can talk in full sentences, but you can't sing.
- **Vigorous intensity:** you can only say a few words before you need to pause for a breath.
- **Too easy for moderate:** you could comfortably sing along to a song.

During a brisk walk, your breathing should be noticeably faster and you may start to feel warm. Your speed will be different from your neighbor's. What matters is the effort for your body, not the number on a watch.

If you like numbers, our guide to [resting heart rate](/heart-health/resting-heart-rate/) shows how to check your pulse, which is a useful way to watch your fitness change over months.

## A 6-week walking plan that starts at 10 minutes

This plan adds a little time each week. Walk at an easy pace for the first few minutes to warm up, then pick up to a brisk pace, and slow down again for the last few minutes. Mayo Clinic recommends warming up and cooling down this way.

| Week | Days per week | Minutes per walk | Weekly total | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5 | 10 | 50 | Just show up. Same time each day if you can |
| 2 | 5 | 15 | 75 | Try the talk test in the middle of each walk |
| 3 | 5 | 20 | 100 | Add one route with a gentle hill or stairs |
| 4 | 5 | 25 | 125 | Split one day into two shorter walks to see how it feels |
| 5 | 5 | 30 | 150 | You've reached the weekly target |
| 6 | 5 | 30 | 150 | Add 2 to 3 short bursts of faster walking inside each walk |

If a week feels too hard, repeat it before moving on. There's no prize for finishing in six weeks. Missing a day isn't a failure either. Pick up where you left off.

## Walking for heart health in your situation

### If you sit at a desk most of the day

Your best walking slots may be the edges of the workday: a walk before you sit down, and one before you go home. Lunch walks and walking phone calls add easy minutes. Our guide to [sitting too much](/heart-health/sitting-too-much-health-risks/) has ideas for breaking up the hours in between.

### If you have high blood pressure

Walking is one of the habits on the standard list for managing blood pressure, alongside eating less sodium and following a heart-healthy eating plan. Keep taking any prescribed medicine, and ask your doctor if there are limits on how hard you should push. Our [guide to lowering blood pressure](/heart-health/how-to-lower-blood-pressure/) puts walking into a four-week habit plan.

### If your joints complain

Choose flat, even surfaces like a track or an indoor hallway, and keep walks shorter but more frequent. Good shoes matter even more here. If pain lasts or gets worse with each walk, have it checked.

### If you have a heart condition or have been inactive for a long time

Talk to your doctor before you start a new activity plan, especially if you have heart disease, have had chest pain, or take medicines that affect your heart rate. Your doctor may suggest a starting point or a supervised program.

## Shoes, weather, and safety on your walks

A few practical details make walking easier to keep up:

- **Shoes:** choose a pair with good support, a firm heel, and enough room for your toes. Replace them when the cushioning feels flat.
- **Hot weather:** walk in the cooler morning or evening hours, bring water, and slow down or stop if you feel dizzy or sick.
- **Cold weather:** wear layers you can take off, cover your head and hands, and watch for ice.
- **Bad weather days:** walk indoors, in a hallway, a large store, or up and down stairs at home.
- **Visibility:** wear bright or reflective clothing if you walk at dawn, dusk, or after dark, and face traffic where there's no sidewalk.
- **Company:** a walking partner, friend, or family member can make it more fun and harder to skip.

## Warning signs to stop walking and get help

Mild breathlessness and tired legs are normal when you're starting out. Some symptoms are not:

- **Call 911** for chest pain, pressure, squeezing, or discomfort, especially if it spreads to your arm, jaw, neck, or back, or comes with shortness of breath, a cold sweat, nausea, or lightheadedness.
- **Call 911** if you faint, or if you have sudden numbness or weakness on one side, trouble speaking, or a sudden severe headache.
- **Stop and call your doctor** if you feel your heart racing, pounding, or skipping in a way that doesn't settle with rest. Our guide to [heart palpitations](/heart-health/heart-palpitations-causes/) explains common causes.
- **See your doctor** if you get unusually short of breath with less effort than before, if your ankles swell, or if leg pain while walking goes away when you stop and returns when you start again.

## Your first walk for heart health, starting today

Put on comfortable shoes and walk for 10 minutes today, easy for the first two, brisk for the middle, and easy for the last two. Try the talk test halfway through. Write the minutes in the log above, and you've started week one.

Walking is one half of the picture. The other half is what happens during the long hours you're not walking, so next, read about the [health risks of sitting too much](/heart-health/sitting-too-much-health-risks/) and easy ways to break them up.
