"Eight glasses a day" is probably the health rule you've heard most often. It's easy to remember. It's also not built for a 6-foot man who mows the lawn in July, works a warehouse shift, or runs on weekends.

**How much water a man should drink depends on his body, activity, and surroundings, but Mayo Clinic reports a general guideline of about 15.5 cups (3.7 liters) of total fluids a day for men, from all drinks and food combined.** That number isn't a target you have to chug in plain water. Roughly a fifth of it usually comes from food, and other drinks count too. For most healthy men, the simplest check is this: you rarely feel thirsty and your urine is pale yellow or colorless.

## Where the 3.7-liter number for men comes from

The figure comes from the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, which set an adequate daily fluid intake for adults. Mayo Clinic summarizes it as:

- **About 15.5 cups (3.7 liters) of fluids a day for men**
- About 11.5 cups (2.7 liters) a day for women

Two details get lost when this number is passed around:

1. **It's total fluid, not just water.** Mayo Clinic notes that about 20 percent of daily fluid usually comes from food. Fruits and vegetables like watermelon, lettuce, and cucumbers are mostly water. Soup counts. So do coffee, tea, and milk.
2. **It's a general guide, not a prescription.** It describes what healthy people tend to take in. It isn't a minimum you'll fail if you miss by a cup.

The old "eight 8-ounce glasses" advice? Mayo Clinic calls it a reasonable goal that's easy to remember, as long as you understand that other drinks and food also add to your total.

## Why one man's water needs differ from another's

MedlinePlus explains that water helps your body keep a normal temperature, cushion joints, protect tissues, and get rid of waste through urine, sweat, and bowel movements. You lose more water when you sweat, breathe hard, or are sick. Mayo Clinic lists several factors that change how much you need:

| What's going on | Effect on your water needs | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exercise or physical work | You lose water through sweat | Drink before, during, and after. Longer, harder sessions need more |
| Hot or humid weather | You sweat more, even sitting still | Start the day with water and keep a bottle within reach |
| High altitude | Can raise fluid loss | Drink a little more than usual on trips to the mountains |
| Fever, vomiting, or diarrhea | You lose fluid fast | Sip often. Ask a doctor about fluids with electrolytes if it lasts |
| Some medical conditions | Heart, kidney, liver, or thyroid problems can make your body hold water | Follow your doctor's fluid advice, which may be lower than average |
| Some medicines | Water pills (diuretics) and others change fluid balance | Ask your doctor or pharmacist what's right for you |

### If you work outside or in a hot job

Construction, roofing, landscaping, kitchens, and warehouses without air conditioning all raise sweat losses. Don't wait for thirst on these days. Drink at regular breaks, keep water close, and watch for dizziness, headache, or very dark urine.

### If you're active or train hard

For a typical 30- to 60-minute workout, drinking water before and after is usually enough. Long endurance events are different: they raise the risk of both dehydration and drinking too much, which is covered below. If you've just started our [strength training plan for men over 40](/mens-health/strength-training-after-40-men/), keep a bottle beside you and sip between sets.

### If you're over 60

MedlinePlus notes that older adults are at higher risk of dehydration. The sense of thirst can weaken with age, and some medicines add to fluid loss. Drinking on a schedule, such as a glass with each meal and one mid-morning and mid-afternoon, can help when thirst isn't a reliable signal.

## The urine color check for hydration

You can't see inside your body, but your bathroom visits give clues. Mayo Clinic says your fluid intake is probably adequate if you rarely feel thirsty and your urine is colorless or light yellow.

| What you notice | What it may mean | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pale yellow or clear, regular trips to the bathroom | Likely well hydrated | Keep your current habits |
| Dark yellow, small amounts | May need more fluid | Drink a glass of water and check again in a few hours |
| Dark urine with dizziness, headache, or dry mouth | Possible dehydration | Drink fluids, rest in the shade or indoors, and call a doctor if it doesn't improve |
| Very large amounts with strong, constant thirst | Could signal a medical problem such as high blood sugar | See your doctor |

Some vitamins, foods, and medicines can change urine color on their own, so look at the pattern over a few days, not just one trip.

## A daily hydration tracker for men

You don't need an app. For three or four days, jot down what you drink and how you feel. Patterns show up fast: the afternoon headache that lines up with zero water after lunch, or the three trips to the bathroom at night after a big evening glass.

| Time | What I drank (and how much) | Activity or weather | Urine color (pale / medium / dark) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example: 7:00 a.m. | Coffee, 12 oz + water, 16 oz | Cool morning | Medium | Woke up thirsty |
| Example: 12:30 p.m. | Water, 20 oz | Mowed lawn, hot | Pale | Felt better after lunch |
| Example: 9:00 p.m. | Water, 16 oz | On couch | Pale | Up twice to pee overnight |
| | | | | |

If your log shows a lot of drinking late in the evening and you're getting up at night to urinate, try moving more of your fluid earlier in the day. Our guide on [peeing at night in men](/mens-health/peeing-at-night-men/) explains other causes worth checking.

### Easy ways to drink a bit more

- Keep a refillable bottle where you work, in your truck, or on your desk
- Have a glass of water with every meal
- Choose water over soda or sweet drinks most of the time, which also cuts sugar and calories
- Add a slice of lemon, lime, or cucumber if plain water bores you
- Eat more fruits, vegetables, and soups, which bring their own water

Alcohol makes you pee more and doesn't help you stay hydrated. If you drink, have water alongside it.

## Can you drink too much water?

Yes, though it's uncommon in healthy adults. Mayo Clinic explains that drinking very large amounts of water can dilute the sodium in your blood, a condition called hyponatremia. It's most often seen in people doing long endurance events, like marathons and triathlons, who drink far more than they sweat out. Certain medical conditions and medicines also raise the risk.

Symptoms can include nausea, vomiting, headache, confusion, tiredness, and muscle cramps or weakness. Severe cases can cause seizures. More isn't always better. Drink to thirst during normal days, and for long events, ask about a plan that matches your sweat losses.

## Thirst and urination changes to tell your doctor about

Make an appointment if you notice:

- Strong thirst that doesn't go away no matter how much you drink
- Peeing much more often or in larger amounts than usual, day or night (MedlinePlus lists increased thirst and frequent urination among the symptoms of diabetes)
- A weak urine stream, trouble starting, or a feeling your bladder isn't empty; these can be prostate-related and are covered in our guide on [an enlarged prostate](/mens-health/enlarged-prostate-back-to-normal/)
- Very little urine for a day or more despite drinking
- Swelling in your legs or sudden weight gain, especially if you have heart or kidney problems
- Dark urine that stays dark even after you drink more

**Get emergency care or call 911** for signs of severe dehydration or heat illness, such as confusion, fainting, a very fast heartbeat, not peeing at all, or a high body temperature with hot skin after heat exposure. Also seek urgent care for confusion, seizures, or repeated vomiting after drinking large amounts of water.

## Your one-glass habit for today

Fill a glass or bottle right now and drink it. Then start the tracker: write down every drink until bedtime and check your urine color three times today. Tomorrow, look at when you drank the least and add one glass there.
