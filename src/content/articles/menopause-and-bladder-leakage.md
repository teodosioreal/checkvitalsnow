Hot flashes and night sweats get most of the attention. But for many women, one of the most frustrating changes of menopause is the bladder. You might need to go more often, feel urges you can't ignore, wake up at night to pee, or leak a little when you cough or rush to the bathroom.

**Menopause can contribute to bladder leakage because lower estrogen affects the tissues of the urethra, bladder, and vagina.** Doctors call the group of vaginal and urinary symptoms caused by this drop in estrogen **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)**. It is common, it tends to continue without treatment, and there are effective ways to manage it.

## How estrogen affects your bladder

Estrogen helps keep the tissues of the vagina, urethra, and bladder thick, elastic, and well supplied with blood. When estrogen levels fall during and after menopause, these tissues change.

According to medical reviews published in PubMed Central, lower estrogen in the urinary tract is linked to:

- Thinner, more fragile tissue lining the urethra
- Weaker contractions of the urethral sphincter, the muscle that holds urine in
- A less elastic bladder

Together, these changes make it harder to hold urine under pressure and easier to feel urgency.

## Bladder symptoms linked to menopause

Urinary symptoms of genitourinary syndrome of menopause can include:

- **Urgency:** a sudden, strong need to urinate
- **Frequency:** needing to go more often than before
- **Nocturia:** waking up at night to urinate
- **Leakage:** with coughing or sneezing (stress incontinence), with urgency (urge incontinence), or both
- **Burning or discomfort** when urinating
- **Repeat urinary tract infections**

Many women also notice vaginal dryness, irritation, or pain during sex, since the same drop in estrogen affects vaginal tissue.

Estimates of how many postmenopausal women have GSM symptoms vary widely in research, but every review agrees it is common. Many women don't mention it to their doctor out of embarrassment, so it often goes untreated.

## Other reasons leakage increases around menopause

Estrogen is only part of the story. Other factors often add up at this stage of life:

- **Pregnancy and childbirth history**, which can weaken the pelvic floor years earlier
- **Natural loss of muscle strength** with age, including the pelvic floor
- **Weight gain**, which adds pressure on the bladder
- **Chronic cough**, constipation, or heavy lifting

The Office on Women's Health notes that pregnancy, childbirth, and menopause are the main reasons urinary incontinence affects twice as many women as men.

## What can help

### Pelvic floor muscle training

Strengthening the pelvic floor helps the muscles hold back urine during pressure and urges. Our guide to [Kegel exercises for women](/womens-health/kegel-exercises-for-women/) shows you how to do them correctly. A pelvic floor physical therapist can check your technique if you are unsure.

### Bladder training and habits

- Go on a schedule and gradually stretch the time between bathroom trips.
- Spread fluids through the day and drink less in the evening.
- Cut back on caffeine, alcohol, and other [common bladder irritants](/womens-health/bladder-irritants-foods-and-drinks/).
- Keep bowels regular with fiber and fluids.

### Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants

A clinical guideline summary notes that non-hormonal moisturizers and lubricants work well for many women with mild vaginal symptoms. They mainly help with dryness and comfort.

### Prescription vaginal estrogen

The same guideline describes low-dose vaginal estrogen as the most effective standard treatment for the genitourinary symptoms of menopause. It is applied locally as a cream, tablet, or ring. Whether it is right for you depends on your health history, so this is a conversation to have with your doctor.

### Other medical options

For overactive bladder or stress incontinence that doesn't improve, doctors have other treatments, including medicines, devices, and procedures. If symptoms don't improve with treatment, guidelines also recommend checking for other causes, such as pelvic floor dysfunction or painful bladder syndrome.

## When to see a doctor

Make an appointment if:

- Bladder symptoms affect your sleep, work, exercise, or social life
- You get urinary tract infections more than once
- You have pain or burning when urinating
- You see blood in your urine
- You notice bleeding after menopause
- You feel a bulge or heaviness in the vagina

Don't assume bladder changes are just part of getting older. Many women feel much better with treatment.

## What else can help

For most women, the strongest plan combines pelvic floor training, bladder-friendly habits, and a conversation with a doctor about medical options such as vaginal estrogen.
