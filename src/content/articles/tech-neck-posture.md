Catch your reflection in a dark phone screen while you scroll and you'll see it: chin dropped, shoulders rounded, head drifting forward like it's trying to get closer to the news. Do that on the couch, at your desk, in line at the store, and in bed before sleep, and it's no surprise your neck and shoulders feel tight by evening.

**"Tech neck" is the strain your neck and upper back muscles take from long stretches of looking down at screens. It is mostly a habit problem, which means the solution is mostly habits too: bring screens up to your eyes, change position often, and strengthen the muscles that hold your head up.** Below you'll find a posture audit with an easy swap for each habit, a three-minute desk routine, and a setup for sleeping.

## Why looking down adds up

Your head is heavy, and your neck muscles hold it up all day. When your head tips forward, those muscles have to work harder to keep it from dropping further. For a minute, it's nothing. For hours a day, every day, the muscles in the back of your neck and the tops of your shoulders get tired, tight, and sore.

Mayo Clinic lists poor posture, such as hunching over a work bench or computer, as a common cause of neck pain. The good news is that this is the most changeable cause there is.

## The posture audit: habits and swaps

Read through the list and note which habits are yours. Then pick two swaps to try this week.

| Habit | Why it strains your neck | Easy swap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Looking down at your phone in your lap | Your head tips far forward for long stretches | Raise the phone toward eye level, and rest your elbow on the table or your other arm |
| Laptop flat on the desk | The screen sits well below your eyes | Raise it on a stand or stack of books and use a separate keyboard and mouse |
| Monitor too low or off to one side | You look down or turn your head for hours | OSHA suggests placing the monitor directly in front of you, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level |
| Phone tucked between ear and shoulder | One side of your neck stays bent | Use speaker, headphones, or a headset, as Mayo Clinic recommends |
| Heavy bag on one shoulder | The same shoulder works all day | Use a backpack with both straps, or switch sides often |
| Hours without moving | Muscles stiffen in one position | Stand and move every 30 to 60 minutes |
| Scrolling in bed propped on pillows | Your chin is pushed toward your chest | Read sitting up with your back supported, or put the phone down earlier |
| Leaning toward the screen | Your head drifts ahead of your shoulders | Sit all the way back in your chair and bring the screen closer |

## A 3-minute desk routine

Do this once in the morning and once in the afternoon. Move slowly, breathe normally, and stop any move that sends pain or tingling into your arm.

| Move | How to do it | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chin tuck | Sit tall. Slide your chin straight back, as if making a double chin. Hold 5 seconds. | 10 |
| Shoulder rolls | Lift your shoulders up toward your ears, roll them back and down. | 10 |
| Shoulder blade squeeze | Squeeze your shoulder blades together and slightly down. Hold 5 seconds. | 10 |
| Side tilt | Tilt one ear toward your shoulder until you feel a gentle stretch. Hold 20 to 30 seconds. | 2 per side |
| Chest opener | Clasp your hands behind your back, gently lift your chest, and look straight ahead. Hold 20 seconds. | 2 |

Chin tucks and shoulder blade squeezes strengthen the muscles that hold your head over your shoulders, so they tend to help more over weeks than a single stretch does in the moment.

## How to sleep so your neck can recover

Hours of sleep in a bent position can undo a day of good habits. Mayo Clinic suggests:

- Keeping your head and neck aligned with your body
- Using a small pillow under your neck
- Sleeping on your back with your thighs raised on pillows, which can help flatten the muscles along your spine

If you sleep on your side, choose a pillow that fills the space between your ear and the mattress so your head doesn't tilt down or up. Stomach sleeping twists the neck to one side for hours, so it's worth trying to change if you wake up sore.

## Which setup fits your day

### If you work on a laptop

The laptop is the biggest single source of looking down. A stand plus an external keyboard and mouse makes the screen eye level without making you reach.

### If you're on your phone most of the day

Bring the phone up, not your head down. For long messages, voice-to-text or a call cuts down on looking down.

### If you're a student

Hours of reading and studying with books flat on a desk add up. Prop books or tablets up on a stand or a stack of books, and take a short break each hour.

### If you already have pain running into your arm

Posture changes still help, but read our guide on [one-sided neck pain](/pain-and-nerves/neck-pain-on-one-side/) for the signs of a pinched nerve and when to see a doctor.

## Signs it's more than posture

See a doctor if neck pain:

- Spreads into your shoulder, arm, or hand with numbness, tingling, or weakness
- Lasts more than a few weeks despite changing your habits
- Comes with headaches that are new or getting worse
- Wakes you up at night

And get emergency care for neck pain after an injury, or a stiff neck with fever and headache.

## Your swap for today

Raise your main screen to eye level today, even if it means a stack of books, and set a reminder to do the 3-minute routine after lunch. Tonight, check your pillow height against the side-sleeper tip above.
