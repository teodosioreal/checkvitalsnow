The car keys turn up in the refrigerator, next to the milk. For a moment, the worry shows up fast: *is this how it starts?*

Probably not. **Occasional memory lapses, like misplacing keys or blanking on a name you remember later, are a normal part of aging. Dementia is different: memory and thinking problems become frequent, keep getting worse, and start to interfere with daily life, such as managing bills or following a conversation.** The question isn't whether you ever forget things. Everyone does. It's whether the forgetting is changing how you live.

That fear is common, and it's a sign you're paying attention, not a sign something is wrong. Below you'll find the side-by-side comparison doctors use, the treatable causes worth ruling out, a table to help you decide when to make an appointment, and what actually happens at a memory evaluation.

## How memory changes with normal aging

As people get older, the brain changes in small ways. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) notes that it can take longer to learn new things, and you may not remember information as well as you once did. Losing your glasses or forgetting a word now and then fits this picture.

What normal aging usually doesn't do is take away your ability to manage your life. You may need a list, a calendar, or an extra minute, but you still get there.

### Mild cognitive impairment: the in-between

Some people have more memory problems than others their age, but can still take care of themselves and handle daily tasks. NIA calls this mild cognitive impairment (MCI). It deserves a doctor's attention because it can raise the chance of developing dementia later, though not everyone with MCI does. Some people stay the same, and some get better.

## Normal aging vs. dementia: NIA's side-by-side

NIA compares typical age-related changes with signs that may point to Alzheimer's disease, the most common cause of dementia. Here it is in plain terms:

| Normal aging | Possible sign of Alzheimer's or another dementia |
| --- | --- |
| Making a bad decision once in a while | Making poor judgments and decisions a lot of the time |
| Missing a monthly payment | Problems taking care of monthly bills |
| Forgetting which day it is and remembering it later | Losing track of the date or time of year |
| Sometimes forgetting which word to use | Trouble having a conversation |
| Losing things from time to time | Misplacing things often and being unable to find them |

The pattern in the right column is about frequency, worsening, and impact. One missed bill is a busy month. Bills going unpaid for several months, when that never used to happen, is a change worth checking.

The Alzheimer's Association lists other early signs, including trouble completing familiar tasks, getting confused about time or place, new problems with words in speaking or writing, withdrawing from work or social activities, and changes in mood and personality.

## Reversible causes of memory loss to check first

Before anyone thinks about dementia, a doctor will look for treatable causes. NIA lists several medical conditions that can cause memory problems which may improve with treatment:

- **Medicine side effects.** Some medicines, or combinations of them, can affect memory and thinking. Bring every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, and supplement to your appointment.
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency.** Low B12 can affect the nervous system and memory. It's more common in older adults and in people who take certain medicines. A simple blood test can check it. Don't start supplements on your own; ask your doctor.
- **Thyroid, kidney, or liver problems.** These can affect thinking and are checked with blood tests.
- **Depression, anxiety, or stress.** NIA notes that emotional problems can make people forgetful, and this kind of forgetfulness often improves when the underlying problem is treated.
- **Heavy alcohol use** over a long period.
- **Other conditions,** such as infections, blood clots, or tumors in the brain, which a doctor may look for depending on symptoms.

Poor sleep belongs on this list too. Sleep problems and sleep apnea can leave you foggy and forgetful during the day. If your main problem is feeling slow and unfocused rather than truly forgetting things, our guide to [brain fog causes](/brain-and-balance/brain-fog-causes/) may fit better. And if you're exhausted all the time, see [chronic fatigue or just tired](/brain-and-balance/chronic-fatigue-or-just-tired/).

Hearing deserves a mention as well. If you didn't clearly hear something, you can't remember it, and missed conversations can look like memory slips. If that sounds familiar, check the [signs of hearing loss](/brain-and-balance/signs-of-hearing-loss/).

## When to see a doctor about memory loss: a decision table

Use this to sort what you or a loved one is noticing. It isn't a diagnosis. It's a way to decide what to do next.

| What you're noticing | Usually fine to watch | Make a doctor's appointment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Names | Blanking on a name, then remembering later | Forgetting names of close family or friends |
| Repeating | Telling a story twice to the same friend once in a while | Asking the same question over and over in one conversation |
| Getting around | Forgetting where you parked at the mall | Getting lost on a familiar route, like driving home |
| Daily tasks | Needing a recipe card for a dish you rarely make | Trouble with a recipe or routine you've done for years |
| Money | An occasional late payment | Unpaid bills, unusual purchases, or falling for scams |
| Mood | Feeling frustrated about forgetting | New withdrawal, suspicion, or personality changes |
| Who notices | Mostly you | Family or friends bring it up, or you've stopped noticing your slips |
| Speed of change | Stable over years | Clearly worse over months |

**Get care the same day** if confusion comes on suddenly over hours or days. Sudden confusion can be caused by an infection, a medicine reaction, low blood sugar, or another medical problem that needs prompt treatment.

**Call 911** if confusion or memory problems come with face drooping, arm or leg weakness, trouble speaking, a sudden severe headache, or trouble walking. These can be signs of a stroke.

## Bringing up memory concerns with a loved one

Noticing changes in a parent or partner is hard, and talking about it can feel even harder. These steps may make the conversation easier:

1. **Write down specific examples first.** "You asked about Sunday dinner three times yesterday" is easier to hear than "You're always forgetting things."
2. **Pick a calm, private moment.** Avoid holidays, crowded rooms, or right after a stressful mix-up.
3. **Lead with care, not alarm.** Try: "I've noticed a few things, and I'd feel better if we checked in with your doctor."
4. **Focus on treatable causes.** Many people are more open to a checkup when it's framed as ruling out a thyroid problem, a vitamin issue, or a medicine side effect.
5. **Offer practical help.** Make the appointment together, drive them there, and offer to sit in.
6. **Don't quiz or argue.** Testing someone on names or dates usually leads to embarrassment, not insight.
7. **Expect more than one conversation.** If it doesn't go well, give it time and try again.

### If you're worried about your own memory

You don't need anyone's permission to ask. Mention it at your next visit, or book one just for this. If you have Medicare, the yearly Annual Wellness Visit includes a check for cognitive changes, which can be a natural place to raise it.

### If you're the caregiver

Ask the person if you can share your observations with their doctor, or send them in writing before the visit. Doctors often find a family member's view very helpful.

## What a cognitive evaluation involves

MedlinePlus explains that diagnosing dementia usually involves several steps, and there's no single test. A typical evaluation may include:

- **A detailed history.** Questions about when the changes began, how they've progressed, and how daily life is going. A family member's input helps here.
- **A medicine review.** Every pill, prescription or not.
- **Short memory and thinking tests.** These might involve remembering a list of words, drawing a clock, or answering questions about the date. They take minutes.
- **A physical and neurological exam.** Checking reflexes, balance, coordination, eyesight, and hearing.
- **Blood tests.** Often including B12 and thyroid levels, among others.
- **Screening for depression.**
- **Brain imaging,** such as a CT or MRI, in some cases.
- **A referral,** if needed, to a neurologist, geriatrician, or neuropsychologist for more detailed testing.

Copy this and fill it in. One example row shows how:

| Date | What happened | How often | Anything new around that time (medicine, illness, stress, poor sleep) |

An early visit has real value even when the answer is reassuring. If a treatable cause turns up, treating it may help. If it's MCI or dementia, an early diagnosis gives time to plan, explore treatments, and get support.

## A first step you can take today

Start the notes table above today, for yourself or for the person you're worried about. Add one example, along with a list of every medicine and supplement taken. Then call the doctor's office and ask for a visit focused on memory. Having the notes ready turns a vague worry into a clear conversation.
