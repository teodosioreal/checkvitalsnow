You bent down to pick up a laundry basket, felt something "go," and now standing straight is a negotiation. Or maybe there was no moment at all: just an ache that crept in over a week of long workdays. Either way, the question in your head is the same. Is this a pulled muscle that will pass, or something like a slipped disc?

**Most lower back pain comes from strained muscles or ligaments and gets better within a few weeks. A herniated disc is more likely when pain shoots down one leg, especially below the knee, or comes with numbness, tingling, or weakness.** The side-by-side check below helps you read your own symptoms, and the last sections cover what helps each and when to stop guessing and see a doctor.

## Why lower backs hurt so often

Your lower back carries much of your upper body's weight and does a lot of bending, lifting, and twisting. Mayo Clinic lists the most common causes as:

- **Muscle or ligament strain**, from lifting something heavy, a sudden awkward move, or poor physical condition
- **Bulging or herniated discs**, when the soft material inside a spinal disc pushes out and presses on a nerve
- **Arthritis** of the spine, which can narrow the space around the spinal cord (spinal stenosis)
- **Weakened bones** from osteoporosis, which can lead to small fractures

Most back pain is short term. NIAMS, part of the National Institutes of Health, notes that most people have back pain at some point, and that acute pain tends to get better on its own over days to weeks.

## Muscle or disc? A side-by-side check

No table replaces an exam, but these patterns help you describe what you're feeling and decide how fast to act.

| Clue | More like a muscle strain | More like a herniated disc pressing on a nerve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it started | After lifting, twisting, or a new activity | Sometimes after lifting, sometimes with no clear trigger |
| Where it hurts | Stays in the lower back, sometimes one side | Lower back plus pain that travels down a leg, often below the knee |
| What it feels like | Sore, tight, achy, sometimes muscle spasms | Sharp, burning, or electric |
| Numbness or tingling | Usually not | Often, in the leg or foot |
| Leg weakness | No | Sometimes (for example, a foot that drags) |
| Worse with | Moving, bending, certain positions | Sitting, coughing, sneezing, bending forward |
| Typical course | Better within a few weeks | Often improves over weeks to months without surgery |

## What helps a strained back muscle

Strained muscles usually heal well with simple care:

- **Keep moving gently.** MedlinePlus notes that bed rest is not recommended for most acute low back pain. Short walks and normal light activity tend to help you recover faster.
- **Cold, then heat.** A cold pack in the first day or two, then heat, can ease pain and stiffness.
- **Over-the-counter pain relievers**, if they are safe for you. Ask a pharmacist if you take other medicines or have kidney, stomach, or heart problems.
- **Ease back into exercise** as pain allows. Strong core and back muscles help protect your spine over time.

## What helps when a disc is involved

Many people with a herniated disc improve without surgery. Mayo Clinic notes that doctors often start with conservative care, such as adjusting activity, pain relievers, and physical therapy. Prescription medicines, injections, or surgery are considered when symptoms are severe, don't improve, or affect nerve function.

At home, the same principles apply: keep gently active, avoid long sitting, and back off any movement that sends pain further down your leg. If sitting is your worst position, our guide to [sciatica when sitting](/pain-and-nerves/sciatica-when-sitting/) shows how to set up your desk and car.

## Who tends to get back pain

Mayo Clinic lists several things that raise the risk. Check the ones that apply to you. These are the areas where small changes can pay off:

- [ ] I'm over 30 to 40
- [ ] I rarely exercise, so my back and core muscles are weak
- [ ] I'm carrying extra weight
- [ ] I lift heavy things with my back instead of my legs
- [ ] I sit for long stretches most days
- [ ] I smoke
- [ ] I'm often anxious or down, which can affect how pain feels

## Warning signs that need a doctor

Mayo Clinic advises seeing a doctor if back pain lasts longer than a few weeks, is severe and doesn't improve with rest, spreads down one or both legs (especially below the knee), causes weakness, numbness, or tingling in one or both legs, or comes with unexplained weight loss.

**Get care right away if back pain:**

- Comes with new bladder or bowel problems
- Comes with a fever
- Follows a fall, a blow to the back, or another injury

These can signal a problem that needs prompt treatment.

## Your next step today

Use the side-by-side table to write down three things: where the pain is, what it feels like, and whether it travels. Then take a slow 5-minute walk and note how you feel afterward. If the pain stays in your back and eases with movement, a strain is likely, and simple care over the next week or two is a good plan. If it travels down your leg, follow the nerve-pain steps and keep an eye on the warning signs.
