Your dad sets down his coffee cup, starts to answer a question, and the words come out wrong. He laughs it off. One corner of his mouth doesn't lift with the laugh. Is that tiredness, or is it something else?

**The signs of a stroke come on suddenly: one side of the face droops, an arm or leg goes weak or numb, speech becomes slurred or confused, vision blurs, balance goes, or a severe headache hits out of nowhere. If you see any of these, call 911 right away and note the time the first symptom started.** You don't need to be sure. Emergency teams would much rather check a false alarm than see a stroke too late.

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a blood vessel in the brain bursts. Brain cells start to die within minutes without oxygen, which is why speed matters more here than almost anywhere else in medicine. Plenty of people hesitate because they don't want to make a fuss. Calling is the right call.

## F.A.S.T.: the four letters behind every stroke check

The CDC and the American Stroke Association both teach F.A.S.T. so anyone can spot a stroke in seconds:

| Letter | What to check | What a stroke can look like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F**ace drooping | Ask the person to smile | One side of the face droops or feels numb, and the smile looks uneven |
| **A**rm weakness | Ask them to raise both arms | One arm drifts downward or can't be lifted, or feels weak or numb |
| **S**peech difficulty | Ask them to repeat a simple sentence | Words are slurred, strange, or hard to understand, or they can't speak |
| **T**ime to call 911 | Don't wait to see if it passes | Call 911 even if symptoms seem to go away, and note the time they started |

### BE FAST adds two more stroke clues

Some hospitals and health systems teach **BE FAST**, which puts two letters in front:

- **B for Balance:** sudden dizziness, loss of balance or coordination, or trouble walking.
- **E for Eyes:** sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes, such as blurred or lost vision.

These two matter because some strokes, especially those in the back of the brain, may not cause obvious face or arm weakness. The CDC's list of stroke symptoms also includes **sudden confusion** and a **sudden severe headache with no known cause**.

## Why the clock time of the first stroke symptom matters

When you call 911, try to notice what time the symptoms started, or the last time the person was seen acting normally. The CDC asks people to do this because some stroke treatments, including clot-dissolving medicine, can only be given within a limited window after symptoms begin. The care team uses that time to decide which treatments are still possible.

A few practical points while you wait for help:

- **Don't drive yourself or the person to the hospital.** Paramedics can start care on the way and alert the hospital's stroke team before you arrive.
- **Don't give food, drink, or medicine**, including aspirin, unless the 911 dispatcher or a medical professional tells you to. Some strokes are caused by bleeding, and the wrong medicine can make that worse.
- **Write down the person's medicines** if you can, or grab the bottles. Blood thinners are especially important to mention.
- **Stay with them** and keep them comfortable, lying on one side if they are drowsy or vomiting.

## A "mini-stroke" is not a small problem

A **transient ischemic attack (TIA)** causes the same symptoms as a stroke, but they go away, often within minutes. People call it a mini-stroke, and it's tempting to shrug it off once you feel normal again.

MedlinePlus describes a TIA as a warning sign that a stroke may follow. Treat it like a stroke: **call 911, even if the symptoms have already faded.** Doctors can't tell a TIA from a stroke just by looking at you, and finding the cause of a TIA gives you a chance to lower the risk of a bigger stroke soon after.

### If stroke symptoms came and went yesterday

Maybe you noticed a numb hand or a few minutes of jumbled words and only now realize what it might have been. Don't wait for it to happen again. Call your doctor today and describe exactly what happened, or go to an emergency room. If any symptoms come back, call 911.

## Stroke signs in different people

Stroke signs follow the same basic pattern in everyone, but a few groups are easy to miss.

### Women

Women have the same core signs as men. The American Stroke Association notes that women may also report less typical symptoms alongside them, such as general weakness, confusion, or feeling unwell. If something suddenly seems wrong, check the F.A.S.T. signs anyway.

### Younger adults

Stroke is more common with age, but it can happen at any age. Sudden one-sided weakness or slurred speech in a 35-year-old needs the same 911 call as in an 80-year-old.

### Someone who lives alone

If you live alone, it helps to know the signs in your own body: sudden numbness on one side, words that won't come out right, or vision that suddenly changes. Keep your phone within reach and call 911 the moment something feels off. Don't call a friend first to ask what they think.

## What raises the risk of stroke

Some risks you can't change, such as getting older, family history, or having had a stroke or TIA before. Many others can be treated or managed. NINDS and the CDC list these as major stroke risk factors:

- **High blood pressure**, the leading controllable risk. Our guide to [high blood pressure symptoms](/heart-health/high-blood-pressure-symptoms/) explains why it often has none.
- **Smoking**
- **Diabetes**
- **High cholesterol**. See what your [cholesterol numbers](/heart-health/high-cholesterol-numbers/) mean.
- **Atrial fibrillation (AFib)**, an irregular heart rhythm that lets clots form in the heart. Learn the [symptoms of AFib](/heart-health/afib-symptoms/).
- **Heart disease**, physical inactivity, and obesity

Knowing your numbers is one of the most useful things you can do. If you haven't had your blood pressure checked in the past year, that's a good place to start.

## Your stroke wallet card

Copy this onto an index card, print it, or save it as a photo on your phone. Keep one in your wallet and one on the fridge, where anyone in your home can see it.

| Check | Ask or look for | Yes / No |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Balance** | Sudden dizziness, stumbling, or trouble walking? | |
| **Eyes** | Sudden blurry, double, or lost vision? | |
| **Face** | Does one side droop when they smile? | |
| **Arms** | Does one arm drift down when both are raised? | |
| **Speech** | Slurred, strange, or missing words? | |
| **Time** | Any "yes" above: call 911 now | |

On the back of the card, write:

- **Time symptoms started:** __________
- **My medicines** (especially blood thinners): __________
- **My doctor's name and phone:** __________
- **Emergency contact:** __________

Filling in the back now saves precious minutes later, when no one is thinking clearly.

## Stroke or heart attack: what if you're not sure?

Some people worry about getting it wrong. You don't need to tell a stroke from a heart attack, a seizure, or very low blood sugar before calling. All of them need emergency care. Call 911 and describe what you see; the dispatcher will guide you.

Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort spreading to the arm, jaw, or back points more toward the heart. Our guide to [heart attack warning signs](/heart-health/heart-attack-warning-signs/) covers those.

## Call 911 for any of these stroke signs

Call 911 right away for any **sudden**:

- Drooping, numbness, or weakness of the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side
- Confusion, trouble speaking, or trouble understanding speech
- Trouble seeing in one or both eyes
- Trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance or coordination
- Severe headache with no known cause

Call even if the symptoms last only a few minutes, and even if the person says they're fine. A short spell can be a TIA, and the next one may not pass.

## Make your stroke card tonight

Take five minutes this evening to fill in your wallet card and walk one other person in your home through the F.A.S.T. checks. Practicing "smile, raise your arms, say this sentence" once feels a little silly. It also means that if the moment ever comes, nobody freezes.
