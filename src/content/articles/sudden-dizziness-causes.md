You roll over in bed and the whole room lurches, spinning for 20 terrifying seconds before it settles. Or you stand up from the couch and everything goes gray at the edges for a moment. Either way, dizziness is frightening, partly because it comes out of nowhere and partly because you can't tell how worried to be.

**Most sudden dizziness comes from the inner ear or from a brief drop in blood pressure, and both are common and usually treatable. The first step is to name the kind of dizzy you feel: spinning (vertigo), faint (lightheadedness), or unsteady.** Each points to different causes. A small number of dizzy spells signal an emergency, and the signs are clear. This guide walks you through all of it, plus what to do the moment a spell hits.

## Three kinds of "dizzy"

MedlinePlus describes dizziness as feeling lightheaded, unsteady, or as though you might faint, and vertigo as feeling that you or the room around you is spinning. Telling them apart is the most useful thing you can do before seeing a doctor.

| What it feels like | What doctors call it | Common causes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The room spins, or you feel like you're spinning | Vertigo | Inner ear problems such as BPPV, inner ear inflammation, or Ménière's disease |
| Faint, woozy, about to pass out, vision graying | Lightheadedness (presyncope) | Drop in blood pressure when standing, dehydration, medicines, low blood sugar, heart rhythm problems |
| Off balance, unsteady on your feet | Disequilibrium | Inner ear problems, nerve problems in the feet, some medicines, vision problems |

## Common causes of spinning (vertigo)

The inner ear holds your balance system, so many vertigo causes start there. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) lists several:

- **Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV).** Mayo Clinic describes it as brief episodes of mild to intense dizziness triggered by specific changes in head position, such as rolling over in bed, lying down, or looking up. Tiny calcium particles drift into a part of the inner ear where they don't belong.
- **Inner ear inflammation** (vestibular neuritis or labyrinthitis), often after a viral infection. Vertigo can be sudden and intense and last days, sometimes with hearing changes.
- **Ménière's disease**, with episodes of vertigo along with hearing loss, ringing in the ear, and a feeling of fullness. If ringing is your main symptom, our guide on [why ears ring](/brain-and-balance/ringing-in-ears-causes/) covers it.
- **Vestibular migraine**, where migraine affects balance, sometimes with or without a headache.

### The Epley maneuver for BPPV

BPPV is often treated with a canalith repositioning procedure, such as the Epley maneuver, a series of slow head and body positions that guides the particles back where they belong. Mayo Clinic notes that a doctor, audiologist, or physical therapist usually performs it and may teach you to do it at home. Because the right side and steps matter, have it shown to you first rather than copying a video.

## Common causes of feeling faint (lightheaded)

- **Standing up quickly**, which can cause a brief drop in blood pressure. Our next guide on [dizziness when standing up](/brain-and-balance/dizzy-when-standing-up/) explains how to prevent it.
- **Dehydration**, from heat, exercise, illness, or not drinking enough
- **Medicines**, especially for blood pressure, water pills, some antidepressants, and sedatives
- **Low blood sugar**, especially if you have diabetes and take insulin or certain pills
- **Heart rhythm problems**, especially if the dizziness comes with a racing or pounding heart

## What to do when a spell hits

1. **Sit or lie down right away**, wherever you are, to avoid falling.
2. **Keep your head still.** For vertigo, focusing on one fixed point can help.
3. **Don't drive**, climb, or operate anything until the spell has fully passed.
4. **Sip water** if you might be dehydrated and aren't nauseated.
5. **Get up slowly** afterward: sit for a minute, then stand while holding something steady.
6. **Note what happened**: what you were doing, how long it lasted, and how it felt.

## Keep a dizziness diary

A diary helps your doctor tell the causes apart. Copy this:

| Date and time | What you were doing | Spinning, faint, or unsteady? | How long it lasted | Other symptoms (nausea, hearing change, racing heart, headache) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 3, 7:10 a.m. | Rolled over in bed | Spinning | About 20 seconds | Mild nausea |
| | | | | |

Patterns like "seconds long, only with head movements" or "always when I stand up" point your doctor quickly in the right direction.

## Matching the pattern to the next step

### If spells last seconds and come with head movements

BPPV is likely. Ask your doctor or a physical therapist about a repositioning maneuver.

### If you feel faint mostly when you stand

Blood pressure and fluids are the first suspects. Read our guide on [dizziness when standing up](/brain-and-balance/dizzy-when-standing-up/).

### If dizziness comes with ringing or hearing changes in one ear

See a doctor. Sudden hearing loss in particular needs prompt care.

### If you're over 65 or take several medicines

Ask your doctor or pharmacist to review your medicines. Falls from dizziness are a real risk at this age.

## Emergency signs: call 911

Mayo Clinic advises getting emergency care for new, severe dizziness or vertigo along with any of these:

- A sudden, severe headache
- Chest pain, or a fast or irregular heartbeat
- Trouble breathing
- Numbness or weakness in the arms or legs, or a drooping face
- Trouble speaking, slurred speech, or confusion
- Double vision or sudden vision changes
- Fainting, or trouble walking
- Ongoing vomiting
- A seizure

MedlinePlus lists sudden dizziness or loss of balance among the warning signs of stroke. When in doubt, call.

## Your step for today

Start the dizziness diary, even if your last spell was days ago. Write down everything you remember about it. Then check your water intake today and stand up slowly every time. If spells keep happening, take the diary to your doctor and say exactly which kind of dizzy you feel.
