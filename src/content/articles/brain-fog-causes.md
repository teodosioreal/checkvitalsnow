You read the same paragraph three times. You walk into the kitchen and forget why. A word you use every day sits on the tip of your tongue and won't come out. It's not that you can't think. It's that thinking feels like walking through mud.

**Brain fog isn't a diagnosis but a description of trouble focusing, slower thinking, and forgetfulness, and it usually has an identifiable cause.** The most common ones are everyday: poor sleep, stress, low mood, and medicines. Others are medical, such as an underactive thyroid, low vitamin B12, hormone changes, or lingering effects of an infection like COVID-19. The trick is figuring out which applies to you, and a two-week log is often the quickest way there.

## What brain fog feels like

People describe it in different ways:

- Trouble concentrating, or focus that drifts after a few minutes
- Forgetting things you just heard or read
- Slow thinking, as if your brain needs more time to load
- Trouble finding the right word
- Feeling mentally tired after simple tasks

The CDC lists "difficulty thinking or concentrating (sometimes referred to as 'brain fog')" among the symptoms of long COVID, which helped bring the phrase into everyday use. But the feeling long predates the pandemic, and most cases have nothing to do with COVID.

## Everyday causes to check first

These are the most common, and the easiest to change:

| Cause | How it can cause fog | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short or poor sleep | The CDC recommends at least 7 hours for adults. Less than that, regularly, affects attention and memory. | Worse after short nights, better after a weekend of rest |
| Ongoing stress | Constant alarm mode makes it hard to focus and often disrupts sleep | Worse during busy, stressful stretches |
| Depression or anxiety | NIMH lists trouble concentrating, remembering, or making decisions among the symptoms of depression | Low mood, loss of interest, constant worry |
| Medicines | Some antihistamines, sleep aids, pain medicines, and others can dull thinking | Started or got worse after a new medicine |
| Alcohol | Even moderate drinking can affect next-day focus and sleep quality | Worse the day after drinking |
| Dehydration and skipped meals | Can leave you sluggish and unfocused | Worse late in the morning or afternoon |

## Medical causes worth testing for

If the everyday causes don't explain it, these are worth a conversation with your doctor:

- **Underactive thyroid.** NIDDK lists trouble thinking or memory problems among possible symptoms of hypothyroidism, along with tiredness, feeling cold, and weight gain.
- **Low vitamin B12.** The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that a deficiency can cause memory problems and confusion, among other symptoms. Older adults, people who don't eat animal foods, and people on some long-term medicines are at higher risk.
- **Perimenopause and menopause.** The Office on Women's Health lists trouble focusing and memory problems among the symptoms some women notice.
- **Long COVID or other post-infection fatigue.** Fog that started after an infection and hasn't lifted is worth mentioning.
- **Sleep apnea.** Loud snoring, gasping at night, or waking with headaches can mean your sleep isn't restful even when it's long enough.

## Track it for two weeks

Brain fog has so many possible causes that a simple log often points to the answer faster than guessing. Rate your fog each evening and note the likely suspects:

| Date | Fog (0–10) | Hours slept | Stress (low, medium, high) | Alcohol or new medicines | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 2 | 7 | 5.5 | High | None | Big deadline, skipped lunch |
| | | | | | |

After two weeks, look for patterns. Do bad days follow short nights? Stressful days? Drinks? If you can't find a pattern, the log is exactly what your doctor needs to decide which tests make sense.

## A two-week plan to clear the fog

While you track, try these changes. They help with many of the everyday causes:

| Week | Focus | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Sleep | Same bedtime and wake time every day, screens off 30 to 60 minutes before bed |
| 1 | Fuel | Regular meals and water through the day |
| 2 | Movement | A brisk 10 to 20 minute walk most days, ideally in daylight |
| 2 | Focus | Work in short blocks with your phone in another room, and write things down instead of holding them in your head |

If your fog lifts by the end of week two, you've found at least part of the cause.

## Which pattern sounds like yours

### If it started after an infection

Fog that began after COVID-19 or another illness and hasn't lifted after a few weeks is worth reporting to your doctor, especially if you also feel wiped out after activity.

### If you're in your 40s or 50s and your periods are changing

Hormone shifts may be part of it. Mention the fog alongside other symptoms such as hot flashes or poor sleep.

### If it comes with dizziness or ringing in your ears

These can share causes such as poor sleep, stress, and some medicines. Our guides on [sudden dizziness](/brain-and-balance/sudden-dizziness-causes/) and [ringing in the ears](/brain-and-balance/ringing-in-ears-causes/) cover them.

### If you take several medicines

Bring a list of everything, including over-the-counter products and supplements, to your doctor or pharmacist and ask whether any of them could affect your thinking.

## When fog needs a doctor, and when it's an emergency

See a doctor if brain fog lasts more than a few weeks, is getting worse, affects your work or safety, or comes with other symptoms such as weight changes, hair loss, low mood, or numbness.

**Call 911 right away** for sudden confusion, trouble speaking or understanding speech, weakness or numbness on one side of the body, a drooping face, or a sudden severe headache. These can be signs of a stroke, and they are not brain fog.

## Tonight's first step

Set a bedtime for tonight that gives you at least seven hours before your alarm, and start the two-week log tomorrow evening. It takes one minute a day, and in two weeks you'll either feel clearer or have a page of real information to take to your doctor.
