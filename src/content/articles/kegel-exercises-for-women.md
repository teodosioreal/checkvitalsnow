Kegel exercises sound simple: squeeze, hold, relax. Yet many women try them for a few days, feel nothing, and give up. Others are never sure they are squeezing the right muscles at all.

**Kegel exercises strengthen your pelvic floor, the group of muscles that supports your bladder, uterus, and bowel.** Done correctly and regularly, they can help with bladder leakage, especially the kind that happens when you sneeze, cough, or laugh. The routine takes only a few minutes a day, and you can do it anywhere without anyone noticing.

Here is how to find the right muscles, a routine you can start today, and the mistakes that hold most women back.

## What is the pelvic floor?

Picture a sling of muscle stretched across the bottom of your pelvis, from your pubic bone in front to your tailbone in back. That is your pelvic floor. It holds your pelvic organs in place and helps you control when urine, gas, and stool come out.

Pregnancy, childbirth, aging, menopause, chronic coughing, and extra weight can stretch or weaken these muscles. When that happens, you may notice [leaking when you sneeze](/womens-health/why-do-i-leak-urine-when-i-sneeze/) or a sudden urge you can't hold.

## Who can benefit from Kegels?

Kegel exercises may help if you:

- Leak urine when you sneeze, cough, laugh, or exercise
- Feel a sudden, strong urge to urinate and sometimes leak
- Leak a few drops after you finish urinating
- Are pregnant or recently gave birth (check with your doctor first)
- Are going through perimenopause or menopause

They may not be right for everyone. Some women have pelvic floor muscles that are too tight, not too weak. If you have pelvic pain, pain with sex, or trouble starting to urinate, ask a doctor before starting.

## Step 1: Find the right muscles

This is the step most women get wrong, so take your time with it. Try one of these methods from NIDDK and Mayo Clinic:

- **Imagine holding in gas.** Tighten the muscles you would use to keep from passing gas in public. That lifting and squeezing feeling is your pelvic floor.
- **Try stopping your urine once.** Midway through urinating, try to slow or stop the flow. The muscles you use are your pelvic floor. Do this only to learn the feeling, not as your regular exercise.
- **Use a finger.** Insert a clean finger into your vagina and squeeze as if holding in urine. If you feel a gentle tightening and lift around your finger, you have found the right muscles.

You should not feel your belly, thighs, or buttocks doing the work. If they tense up, relax and try again with a smaller squeeze.

## Step 2: A simple starter routine

Once you can find the muscles, follow this basic routine:

1. **Empty your bladder** first.
2. **Lie down** on your back with your knees bent. This is the easiest position to start.
3. **Squeeze** your pelvic floor muscles and hold for **3 seconds**.
4. **Relax fully** for **3 seconds**. The relaxing part is as important as the squeeze.
5. **Repeat 10 times.**
6. Do this routine **about three times a day.**

As you get stronger, slowly work up to holding for 5 to 10 seconds, with the same amount of time to relax. Aim for 10 to 15 repetitions per session.

### Add different positions

Once lying down feels easy, practice while sitting and standing too. NIDDK notes that exercising in all three positions builds the most strength, because your muscles have to work against gravity during daily life.

### Add quick squeezes

Some routines also include fast "flicks": squeeze hard for one second, then let go. Quick squeezes train the muscles to react fast, which helps when a sneeze or cough catches you off guard.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Holding your breath.** Breathe normally the whole time.
- **Squeezing the wrong muscles.** Your stomach, thighs, and buttocks should stay relaxed.
- **Bearing down instead of lifting.** A Kegel should feel like squeezing and lifting up, not pushing down.
- **Skipping the relaxation.** Muscles get stronger when they fully contract and fully relax.
- **Overdoing it.** More is not always better. Tired muscles can make leakage worse for a while.
- **Using midstream stopping as exercise.** Stopping urine regularly can keep the bladder from emptying fully and may raise the risk of a bladder infection.

## How long until Kegels work?

Be patient. Muscle strength builds slowly. Many women need several weeks of steady practice, and some need a few months, before they notice less leakage. The women who see results tend to be the ones who stick with the routine every day.

A simple way to stay consistent: link your Kegels to something you already do. Do a set at every red light, while brushing your teeth, or during the commercials of a show.

## Getting help if you are not sure

Many women find it hard to tell whether they are doing Kegels right. That is normal. A doctor, nurse, or **pelvic floor physical therapist** can check your technique. They may use **biofeedback**, where a small sensor shows on a screen when you are squeezing the right muscles.

## When to see a doctor

Talk to a doctor if:

- You have done Kegels correctly for a few months and see no change
- Kegels cause pain
- Your leakage is getting worse
- You have pain or burning when you urinate, blood in your urine, or fever
- You feel heaviness or a bulge in your vagina

## What else can help

Kegels work best as part of a bigger plan: steady practice, a few [bladder-friendly habits](/womens-health/bladder-irritants-foods-and-drinks/), and a check-up when something changes.
