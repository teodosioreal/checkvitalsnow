Ten minutes into a meeting, it starts: a deep ache in one buttock, then that familiar burn down the back of your leg. You shift. You cross your legs, then uncross them. By the end of a long drive, getting out of the car feels like a project.

**Sciatica often hurts more when you sit because sitting adds load to the lower spine and can press on the area where the sciatic nerve runs. The two biggest levers you control are how you sit and how long you sit without a break.** Below, you'll find a desk setup checklist, a car setup, and a break routine that fits into a real workday.

## Why sitting makes the nerve flare

Most sciatica starts in the lower spine, where a bulging or herniated disk or a narrowed space presses on a nerve root. Sitting, especially slumped forward, increases the pressure on those disks compared with standing or lying down. If a disk is already pushing on a nerve, that extra load can turn a dull ache into a sharp flare.

There's a second, simpler reason too: the sciatic nerve passes deep through the buttock. A hard seat, a wallet in your back pocket, or sitting on one hip can add pressure right where it runs.

Mayo Clinic lists long periods of sitting, and an inactive lifestyle in general, among the risk factors for sciatica. That doesn't mean your chair caused it, but it does mean your chair can keep it going.

## The desk setup checklist

Run through this list once at your desk. Most fixes cost nothing. The positions follow OSHA's guidance on neutral sitting postures at a computer.

- [ ] I sit all the way back so the chair supports my lower back
- [ ] There's a small cushion or rolled towel behind the curve of my lower back
- [ ] My feet rest flat on the floor or on a footrest
- [ ] My hips are about level with, or slightly higher than, my knees
- [ ] The screen is about at eye level, so I'm not leaning forward
- [ ] My keyboard and mouse are close, so I'm not reaching
- [ ] My wallet and phone are in a front pocket or on the desk
- [ ] I'm not sitting with my legs crossed for long stretches

If you checked fewer than six, start with the lower back support and the feet. They tend to make the biggest difference.

## A break routine that fits a workday

A good chair helps, but no chair fixes sitting for three hours straight. Changing position often is what keeps the nerve calmer. Here's a simple rhythm:

| Every… | Do this | Time it takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 to 60 minutes | Stand up, walk to get water or to the window and back | 1 to 2 minutes |
| 2 hours | A short walk plus one standing back bend: hands on your lower back, gently lean back a little | 3 to 5 minutes |
| Lunch | A 10-minute walk outside if you can | 10 minutes |
| End of day | The gentle stretches from our [sciatica home-care guide](/pain-and-nerves/sciatica-pain-relief-at-home/) | 5 minutes |

Tie your breaks to things that already happen, such as standing for every phone call or refilling water after each meeting. That way you don't have to rely on memory.

### What about a standing desk?

Standing all day isn't the answer either, since it brings its own strain on the back and legs. What tends to help is **switching**: some sitting, some standing, and walking in between. If you have a sit-stand desk, change position every 30 to 60 minutes.

## How to sit in the car

Long drives are a common trigger. Before your next trip:

- **Move the seat closer** so you don't have to reach for the pedals, which can tilt the pelvis.
- **Raise the back of the seat to fairly upright**, slightly reclined, rather than leaning far back.
- **Add support** for your lower back with a small pillow or rolled towel.
- **Take the wallet out** of your back pocket.
- **Stop every hour or so** on long trips to walk for a few minutes.

Getting out of the car can be the worst moment. Turn your whole body, put both feet on the ground, then stand up using your legs and the door frame.

## Which tips fit your situation

### If you work from home on a couch or kitchen chair

A soft couch lets you sink and slump. Move your laptop to a table, use a chair with a firm back, and stack books to raise the screen.

### If you drive for work

Plan stops every hour, keep a small back cushion in the car, and do a standing back bend at each stop.

### If you're pregnant

Back and leg pain are common in pregnancy, and sitting habits help here too. Ask your doctor or midwife before starting any new stretches, and mention any numbness or weakness.

### If sitting is the only time it hurts

That pattern is common with disk-related sciatica. Focusing on breaks and support often brings the most relief.

## When sitting pain needs a doctor

See a doctor if pain lasts more than a week despite these changes, keeps getting worse, or wakes you at night. **Get emergency care right away** if you have sudden weakness or numbness in a leg, or trouble controlling your bladder or bowels. Mayo Clinic lists these as signs that need immediate attention.

## Try this at your desk today

Put a rolled towel behind your lower back, move your wallet to your front pocket, and set a 45-minute timer for a standing break. Do that for one workday and notice how your leg feels by evening compared with yesterday.
